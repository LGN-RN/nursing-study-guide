1. Learning Objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

  • Describe processes that regulate fluid distribution, extracellular fluid volume (ECV), and body fluid osmolality.
  • Explain processes that regulate electrolyte and acid-base balance.
  • Recall common fluid, electrolyte, and acid-base imbalances including their causes and signs/symptoms.
  • Identify risk factors for fluid, electrolyte, and acid-base imbalances.
  • Apply the nursing process when caring for patients with these imbalances.
  • Explain the rationale and procedures for initiating and maintaining IV therapy.
  • Identify complications of IV therapy and appropriate nursing actions.
  • Describe the procedure for initiating and monitoring a blood transfusion and nursing actions for transfusion reactions.
  • Evaluate outcomes of care for patients with fluid, electrolyte, and acid-base imbalances.
2. Key Terms
Acidosis
A condition causing blood to become relatively too acidic (pH < 7.35); two types: respiratory and metabolic.
Alkalosis
A condition causing blood to become relatively too alkaline (pH > 7.45); two types: respiratory and metabolic.
Anion gap
Na+ − (Cl− + HCO3−); reflects unmeasured anions. Normal: 6 ± 4 mEq/L. Elevated anion gap indicates excess metabolic acids (e.g., lactic acidosis, DKA).
Arterial blood gases (ABGs)
Lab tests on arterial blood measuring pH, PaCO2, HCO3−, PaO2, and SaO2 to evaluate acid-base and oxygenation status.
Autologous transfusion
Collection and reinfusion of a patient's own blood; reduces risk of transfusion reactions and bloodborne pathogen exposure.
Buffers
Chemical pairs that resist pH change by accepting or releasing H+ ions. The major ECF buffer is the bicarbonate (HCO3−) system (maintained at a 20:1 ratio with carbonic acid).
Colloid osmotic pressure (oncotic pressure)
Inward-pulling pressure from blood proteins (mainly albumin) that draws fluid from the interstitial space back into capillaries.
Colloids
Large protein molecules in blood (e.g., albumin) that do not readily leave capillaries; used as IV volume expanders.
Crystalloids
IV solutions containing water, electrolytes, and/or dextrose (e.g., normal saline, Lactated Ringer's); distribute throughout the ECF.
Diffusion
Passive movement of particles from areas of higher to lower concentration; requires ion channels for electrolytes to cross cell membranes.
Extracellular fluid (ECF)
Fluid outside cells (~1/3 of total body water); includes plasma (intravascular) and interstitial fluid.
Extracellular volume (ECV) deficit
Insufficient isotonic fluid in the ECF; also called hypovolemia when vascular volume is reduced.
Extracellular volume (ECV) excess
Too much isotonic fluid in the ECF; produces edema, weight gain, and crackles in the lungs.
Extravasation
IV infiltration of a vesicant (tissue-damaging) drug; requires immediate intervention to prevent necrosis.
Filtration
Net movement of fluid between capillaries and interstitial space driven by the balance of hydrostatic and osmotic pressures (Starling forces).
Hydrostatic pressure
The force of fluid pressing outward against a surface; capillary hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out of capillaries.
Hypercalcemia / Hypocalcemia
Abnormally high or low serum calcium. Normal total: 9.0–10.5 mg/dL; ionized: 4.5–5.6 mg/dL.
Hyperkalemia / Hypokalemia
Abnormally high or low serum potassium. Normal: 3.5–5.0 mEq/L. Both can cause life-threatening dysrhythmias.
Hypermagnesemia / Hypomagnesemia
Abnormally high or low serum magnesium. Normal: 1.3–2.1 mEq/L.
Hypernatremia
Water deficit; serum Na+ > 145 mEq/L; body fluids too concentrated (hyperosmolar); causes brain cells to shrink.
Hypertonic
A solution more concentrated than normal body fluids (> ~295 mOsm/kg); draws water out of cells.
Hyponatremia
Water excess; serum Na+ < 136 mEq/L; body fluids too dilute (hypoosmolar); causes cells to swell.
Hypotonic
A solution less concentrated than normal body fluids; water moves into cells.
Hypovolemia
Decreased vascular volume; often used synonymously with ECV deficit.
Infiltration
Leakage of non-vesicant IV fluid into subcutaneous tissue from a dislodged catheter or ruptured vein.
Interstitial fluid
Fluid between cells and outside blood vessels; the larger portion of ECF (~11 L).
Intracellular fluid (ICF)
Fluid inside cells; ~2/3 of total body water (~28 L).
Isotonic
A solution with the same tonicity as normal body fluids (~280 mOsm/kg); no net osmotic water shift.
Metabolic acidosis
Excess metabolic acids or loss of bicarbonate; pH < 7.35, HCO3− < 21 mEq/L.
Metabolic alkalosis
Deficit of metabolic acids or bicarbonate excess; pH > 7.45, HCO3− > 28 mEq/L.
Osmolality
Number of particles per kilogram of water. Normal serum: 285–295 mOsm/kg. Drives ADH release and thirst.
Osmosis
Movement of water through a semipermeable membrane from lower to higher particle concentration.
Osmotic pressure
Inward-pulling force created by dissolved particles; the compartment with higher osmotic pressure draws water toward it.
Phlebitis
Inflammation of the inner layer (intima) of a vein; signs include redness, warmth, tenderness, and a palpable cord.
Respiratory acidosis
Excess carbonic acid from alveolar hypoventilation; pH < 7.35, PaCO2 > 45 mm Hg.
Respiratory alkalosis
Deficit of carbonic acid from alveolar hyperventilation; pH > 7.45, PaCO2 < 35 mm Hg.
Transfusion reaction
Immune response to incompatible blood components; ranges from mild (febrile, allergic) to life-threatening (acute hemolytic).
Vascular access devices (VADs)
Catheters or ports for repeated venous access; includes short peripheral IVs, PICC lines, and central venous catheters.

Maintaining these concentration differences between compartments is essential for normal nerve and muscle function.

Mechanisms of Fluid & Electrolyte Movement

Active Transport
Uses ATP energy to move electrolytes against a concentration gradient. Classic example: the sodium-potassium (Na+-K+) pump moves Na+ out of cells and K+ into cells.
Diffusion
Passive movement of particles down a concentration gradient (high → low). Electrolytes need ion channels to diffuse across cell membranes; the opening of these channels is tightly controlled.
Osmosis
Water moves through a semipermeable membrane toward the compartment with higher osmotic pressure (more particles). Cell membranes freely permit water movement but restrict most solutes, so even small osmolality differences shift large volumes of water.
Filtration (Starling Forces)
Net fluid movement between capillaries and the interstitium is governed by four forces:
  • Capillary hydrostatic pressure — pushes fluid out of capillaries (strongest at arterial end)
  • Blood colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure — pulls fluid into capillaries (protein-driven; strongest at venous end)
  • Interstitial hydrostatic pressure — weak force pushing fluid into capillaries
  • Interstitial colloid osmotic pressure — normally very small; pulls fluid out of capillaries
Lymphatics drain any excess interstitial fluid and proteins back to circulation.
Clinical Pearl — Edema Edema forms when capillary hydrostatic pressure rises (heart failure, venous obstruction), plasma oncotic pressure falls (hypoalbuminemia, cirrhosis), or capillary permeability increases (inflammation, burns, allergic reactions).

Thirst Mechanism

Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect rising plasma osmolality or falling blood volume and trigger thirst. Key limitation: infants, older adults, confused, or obtunded patients may not perceive or communicate thirst — do NOT rely on thirst alone to assess hydration status in these populations.

6. Electrolytes & Their Imbalances

Potassium (K+) — Critical for Cardiac & Muscle Function

Hypokalemia (K+ < 3.5 mEq/L)Hyperkalemia (K+ > 5.0 mEq/L)
Causes Diarrhea, vomiting, K+-wasting diuretics (furosemide, thiazides), alkalosis, insulin therapy for DKA, inadequate IV potassium, corticosteroids, aldosterone excess Oliguria/renal failure, K+-sparing diuretics, massive cell destruction (crush injury, chemo), DKA with insufficient insulin, some acidoses, ACE inhibitors, excess IV K+, stored blood transfusion
Signs & Symptoms Bilateral muscle weakness (starts in quadriceps, may ascend to respiratory muscles), abdominal distention, hypoactive bowel sounds, constipation, dysrhythmias Bilateral muscle weakness in quadriceps, transient abdominal cramps, diarrhea, dysrhythmias, cardiac arrest if severe
ECG Changes U waves, flattened or inverted T waves, ST depression Peaked T waves, widened QRS, prolonged PR interval, terminal sine-wave pattern
Critical Safety — Potassium NEVER administer KCl by IV push — fatal cardiac dysrhythmia can result. Always verify adequate urine output (≥30 mL/hr) before administering K+-containing IV solutions. The body cannot conserve K+; patients receiving nothing by mouth will develop hypokalemia without K+ supplementation.

Calcium (Ca2+) — Controls Neuromuscular Excitability

Hypocalcemia (Total < 9.0 mg/dL)Hypercalcemia (Total > 10.5 mg/dL)
Causes Vitamin D deficiency, hypoparathyroidism, pancreatitis, chronic diarrhea/steatorrhea, hypoalbuminemia, alkalosis, hyperphosphatemia, end-stage renal disease, rapid citrated blood transfusion Prolonged immobilization, hyperparathyroidism, bone tumors, cancers secreting bone-resorbing factors (lung, breast), thiazide diuretics, milk-alkali syndrome
Signs & Symptoms Increased excitability: numbness/tingling (fingers, toes, circumoral), positive Chvostek sign, hyperactive DTRs, muscle twitching/cramping, tetany, laryngospasm, seizures, dysrhythmias Decreased excitability: anorexia, nausea/vomiting, constipation, fatigue, diminished reflexes, lethargy, confusion, cardiac arrest if severe; pathological fractures
Hypocalcemia — "CATS" Convulsions • Arrhythmias • Tetany • Spasms/Stridor (laryngospasm)

Hypercalcemia — "Stones, Bones, Groans, Moans" Kidney stones • Bone pain/fractures • GI groans (constipation, N/V) • Moans (confusion, lethargy)

Magnesium (Mg2+) — Mirrors Calcium Effects; Important Enzyme Cofactor

Hypomagnesemia (< 1.3 mEq/L)Hypermagnesemia (> 2.1 mEq/L)
Causes Malnutrition, chronic alcoholism, diarrhea, laxative overuse, thiazide/loop diuretics, steatorrhea, aldosterone excess, rapid citrated blood transfusion End-stage renal disease (oliguric), excessive Mg2+ antacids or laxatives, adrenal insufficiency
Signs & Symptoms Similar to hypocalcemia: positive Chvostek sign, hyperactive DTRs, muscle cramps/twitching, tetany, seizures; also insomnia, tachycardia, hypertension, dysrhythmias, tremors Lethargy, hypoactive DTRs, bradycardia, hypotension; severe: decreased rate/depth of respirations, dysrhythmias, cardiac arrest
Nursing Alert — Magnesium & Other Electrolytes Hypomagnesemia frequently coexists with hypokalemia and hypocalcemia and can make these imbalances refractory to treatment. Always check and correct magnesium when treating persistent hypokalemia or hypocalcemia.

7. Acid-Base Balance

ParameterNormal RangeWhat It Measures

HCO3−21–28 mEq/LBicarbonate concentration; the metabolic/renal component
PaO280–100 mm HgO2 partial pressure; measures oxygenation

Base excess−2 to +2 mEq/LBuffering capacity; < −2 = excess metabolic acids; > +2 = excess bicarbonate

Acid Production, Buffering & Excretion

  • Acid production: Cellular metabolism constantly produces carbonic acid (CO2 + H2O → H2CO3 → H+ + HCO3−) and metabolic acids (lactic, citric, keto acids).
  • Buffers: Work within seconds. Major ECF buffer: HCO3−/H2CO3 system (20:1 ratio). Others: hemoglobin, proteins, phosphate, cellular and bone buffers.
  • Lungs: Excrete carbonic acid as CO2 + H2O. Response time: minutes. Rate/depth of breathing adjusts PaCO2.
  • Kidneys: Excrete all metabolic acids; reabsorb or excrete HCO3−. Response time: 24–72 hours to reach full compensation. Phosphate buffers and NH4+ formation protect urine from becoming too acidic.

The Four Acid-Base Imbalances

DisorderPrimary ChangeCommon CausesCompensationABG Pattern (uncompensated)
Respiratory Acidosis CO2 accumulates (hypoventilation) COPD, pneumonia, airway obstruction, respiratory muscle weakness, drug overdose (respiratory depressant), head injury Kidneys retain HCO3−; slow (24 h–5 days) pH <7.35 ↓, PaCO2 >45 ↑, HCO3− normal
Respiratory Alkalosis CO2 blown off (hyperventilation) Hypoxemia, acute pain, anxiety, mechanical ventilator settings, gram-negative sepsis, aspirin overdose Kidneys excrete HCO3−; usually short-lived pH >7.45 ↑, PaCO2 <35 ↓, HCO3− normal
Metabolic Acidosis HCO3− decreases DKA, lactic acidosis (circulatory shock), oliguric renal disease, diarrhea (HCO3− loss), toxin ingestion (methanol, ethylene glycol) Lungs hyperventilate (Kussmaul breathing); begins within minutes pH <7.35 ↓, HCO3− <21 ↓, PaCO2 normal
Metabolic Alkalosis HCO3− increases Vomiting, gastric suction, hypokalemia, excess aldosterone, sodium bicarbonate overdose, massive transfusion Lungs hypoventilate; limited by hypoxia drive pH >7.45 ↑, HCO3− >28 ↑, PaCO2 normal
ABG Interpretation — ROME Method
  1. Check pH: <7.35 = acidosis; >7.45 = alkalosis
  2. Check PaCO2: if it moved opposite to pH → Respiratory cause
  3. Check HCO3−: if it moved same direction as pH → Metabolic cause
  4. Is compensation present? The body corrects to normal pH, not to normal PaCO2 or HCO3−
ROME = Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal (compared to pH direction)

Intake & Output (I&O) Monitoring

  • 1 kg weight change overnight ≈ 1 L of fluid gained or lost.
  • 24-hour I&O should be approximately equal in a patient with normal fluid balance.
  • Intake: oral fluids, food water, IV fluids, tube feedings, liquid medications, irrigation fluids.
  • Output: urine, diarrhea, vomit, NG suction drainage, wound drainage.
  • Weigh patient at the same time each day, same scale, same clothing, after voiding.
  • Normal urine output: ≥30 mL/hr for adults. Report output below this threshold.

Priority Nursing Diagnoses

  • Fluid Imbalance / Dehydration (related to GI losses, fever, inadequate intake)
  • Electrolyte Imbalance
  • Acid-Base Imbalance
  • Lack of Knowledge of Fluid Regimen
  • Risk for Impaired Skin Integrity (from persistent diarrhea or edema)

Vascular Access Devices (VADs)

  • Short peripheral IV catheter: Short-term use; upper extremity veins preferred; over-the-needle design; common gauges 20 and 22 (larger gauge number = smaller diameter).
  • PICC line: Peripherally inserted central catheter; enters arm vein; tip in superior vena cava; long-term use.
  • Central venous catheter (CVC): Tip near right atrium; for large volumes, parenteral nutrition (PN), and irritating/vesicant medications.
  • Implanted port: Subcutaneous; for long-term intermittent access (e.g., chemotherapy).

Key IV Insertion & Maintenance Points

  1. Verify order; identify patient using two identifiers.
  2. Check IV solution label: name, concentration, additives, volume, expiration date, clarity.
  3. Prime tubing completely — remove all air (large air bubbles can cause air embolism).
  4. Apply tourniquet 10–15 cm above site; cleanse site with antiseptic; allow to dry fully.
  5. Insert catheter bevel-up; look for blood flashback; advance catheter into vein while withdrawing stylet.
  6. Secure catheter; apply sterile transparent dressing; label with date and time.
  7. Assess IV site every 1–4 hours (every 1–2 hours for critically ill or sedated patients).

CLABSI Prevention Bundle

  • Hand hygiene before insertion and every line manipulation
  • Maximum sterile barrier precautions during CVC insertion
  • Chlorhexidine skin antisepsis (≥0.5% with alcohol)
  • Optimal site selection (subclavian preferred over femoral for adults)
  • Daily review of line necessity; remove promptly when no longer needed

Safety — Pre-Transfusion Checklist
  1. Verify provider order.
  2. Obtain type and crossmatch; confirm with blood bank slip.
  3. Two nurses verify: patient ID (2 identifiers), blood bag label, blood type, unit number, expiration date, Rh factor.
  4. Obtain baseline vital signs; recheck at 15 minutes after start, then per protocol.
  5. Use ≥18-gauge IV with blood tubing (inline filter); flush only with 0.9% NS — never D5W.
  6. Infuse first 15–50 mL slowly; stay at bedside for first 15 minutes (highest reaction risk).
11. Review Questions

  1. A patient with chronic diarrhea and vomiting for 3 days presents with postural hypotension, tachycardia, and dark yellow urine. Which fluid imbalance is most likely, and what lab changes would you expect?

    Show Answer
    ECV deficit (hypovolemia), likely with clinical dehydration. Lab changes: elevated hematocrit (hemoconcentration), BUN >20 mg/dL, urine specific gravity >1.030. If more water than Na+ is lost, serum Na+ >145 mEq/L and serum osmolality >295 mOsm/kg indicate concurrent hypernatremia (clinical dehydration).

  2. ABG results: pH 7.30, PaCO2 52 mm Hg, HCO3− 25 mEq/L. What is the disorder? Is compensation present?

    Show Answer
    Respiratory acidosis, uncompensated. pH <7.35 (acidosis); PaCO2 >45 (elevated — respiratory cause); HCO3− is normal (kidneys have not yet compensated). The kidneys would eventually raise HCO3− above 28 mEq/L over 24–72 hours to partially restore pH.

  3. Why must urine output be verified before giving a K+-containing IV solution?

    Show Answer
    The kidneys are the primary route of K+ excretion. If the patient is oliguric or anuric, K+ cannot be excreted; administering more K+ causes hyperkalemia, which leads to fatal cardiac dysrhythmias and cardiac arrest. KCl must never be given by IV push.

  4. A patient with end-stage renal disease is likely to develop which electrolyte imbalances? Why?

    Show Answer
    Oliguric end-stage renal disease impairs excretion of all electrolytes and metabolic acids. Expected imbalances: hyperkalemia, hypermagnesemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia (kidneys cannot activate vitamin D), metabolic acidosis (H+ cannot be excreted), and ECV excess (Na+ and H2O retained).

  5. What is the difference between infiltration and extravasation? How would you manage each?

    Show Answer
    Infiltration: non-vesicant IV fluid leaks into subcutaneous tissue. Management: stop infusion, discontinue IV, elevate extremity, apply warm/cold compress per protocol, restart elsewhere.

    Extravasation: a vesicant (tissue-damaging) drug leaks in; risks necrosis. Management: stop infusion, disconnect tubing, aspirate drug from catheter before removal, administer antidote per policy, notify provider immediately. Do NOT apply pressure.

  6. A patient receiving a blood transfusion develops low back pain, chills, hypotension, and pink-tinged urine within 15 minutes. What is happening? What do you do?

    Show Answer
    Suspected acute hemolytic transfusion reaction (ABO incompatibility — most dangerous). Immediate actions: STOP the transfusion immediately; keep IV open with 0.9% NS; notify provider and blood bank; send remaining blood bag, tubing, and new patient blood sample to the lab; monitor vital signs; assess urine hourly (hemoglobinuria); support blood pressure; document everything.

  7. Describe the compensatory mechanism for metabolic acidosis. How do you recognize compensation on an ABG?

    Show Answer
    In metabolic acidosis, HCO3− falls. The lungs compensate by hyperventilating (Kussmaul breathing) to blow off CO2, which lowers PaCO2 and raises pH toward normal. Begins within minutes. ABG pattern: pH <7.35, HCO3− <21 (metabolic cause), PaCO2 <35 (compensatory respiratory response).

  8. A patient with SIADH is at risk for which imbalance? What signs would you assess for?

    Show Answer
    SIADH (excess ADH) causes H2O retention → hyponatremia (water excess, hypoosmolar). Serum Na+ <136 mEq/L. Signs: decreased LOC (confusion, lethargy, coma), seizures if onset is rapid or Na+ is severely low; brain cells swell. Monitor serum sodium, osmolality, and neurological status closely.

  9. Why are older adults particularly vulnerable to fluid imbalances?

    Show Answer
    Older adults have: decreased total body water (~50% vs 60%); decreased thirst sensation; decreased renal concentrating ability; multiple medications (diuretics, ACE inhibitors); chronic diseases; impaired mobility (may restrict fluids). Do not rely on thirst — monitor weights, I&O, labs, and clinical signs.

  10. What IV solution would be ordered for ECV deficit? For hypernatremia? Explain the rationale.

    Show Answer
    ECV deficit: Isotonic Na+-containing solution (0.9% NaCl or Lactated Ringer's) — stays in ECF to restore vascular and interstitial volume without shifting H2O into or out of cells.

    Hypernatremia: Hypotonic solution (0.45% NaCl or D5W) — dilutes the concentrated ECF and allows H2O to rehydrate cells by osmosis. Correction must be gradual to prevent cerebral edema.

  11. Why do hypomagnesemia and hypocalcemia produce similar signs and symptoms?

    Show Answer
    Both Ca2+ and Mg2+ normally decrease neuromuscular excitability. Deficits of either cause increased neuromuscular excitability: Chvostek sign, hyperactive DTRs, muscle twitching, tetany, paresthesias, seizures. Also, hypomagnesemia often causes concurrent hypocalcemia and hypokalemia, making these co-occurring imbalances refractory to treatment until Mg2+ is replaced.

  12. Define phlebitis. List three risk factors for its development at a peripheral IV site.

    Show Answer
    Phlebitis: inflammation of the inner layer (intima) of a vein. Signs: redness, warmth, tenderness, streak formation, palpable venous cord along the catheter path.

    Risk factors (any three): acidic or hypertonic IV solutions; rapid infusion rate; irritating drugs (KCl, vancomycin, penicillin); catheter placed in area of flexion; poorly secured catheter; poor hand hygiene or lack of aseptic technique; catheter too large for the vein.

13. ⚑ NCLEX Critical To Knows

These are the highest-yield facts for NCLEX-style questions on this chapter. Master these first.

● Fluid Assessment Priorities NCLEX
  • Daily weight is the most reliable indicator of fluid status. 1 kg gained or lost overnight = 1 L of fluid.
  • Normal urine output = ≥30 mL/hr for adults. Output <30 mL/hr must be reported.
  • Postural (orthostatic) hypotension = strong sign of ECV deficit. Have patient sit before standing to prevent falls.
  • Dry mucous membrane between the cheek and gum is more reliable than dry lips for detecting ECV deficit.
  • Do NOT rely on thirst in infants, older adults, confused patients, or patients with altered LOC — they may be severely dehydrated without feeling thirsty.
  • Skin turgor is unreliable in older adults (skin loses elasticity with aging).
  • ECV excess signs: crackles, JVD when upright, sudden weight gain, dependent edema — think heart failure or excessive Na+-containing fluids.
● Potassium — The Most Tested Electrolyte NCLEX
  • NEVER give KCl IV push — cardiac arrest. This is a classic NCLEX trick.
  • Always check urine output (≥30 mL/hr) before giving K+ IV. No output = no potassium.
  • Hypokalemia ECG: U waves and flattened/inverted T waves.
  • Hyperkalemia ECG: Peaked (tall, tented) T waves → widened QRS → sine wave → cardiac arrest.
  • Loop diuretics (furosemide) and thiazides waste K+. Patients on these need monitoring and often supplementation.
  • K+-sparing diuretics (spironolactone) + ACE inhibitors together = high risk for hyperkalemia.
  • Patients on digoxin are at much higher risk for digoxin toxicity when hypokalemic — K+ competes with digoxin at receptor sites.
● Calcium & Magnesium Imbalances NCLEX
  • Chvostek sign: Tap the facial nerve just anterior to the ear → ipsilateral facial muscle twitching. Positive = hypocalcemia or hypomagnesemia.
  • Trousseau sign: Inflate BP cuff above systolic for 3 minutes → carpal spasm. Positive = hypocalcemia.
  • Hypocalcemia = increased neuromuscular excitability (CATS: Convulsions, Arrhythmias, Tetany, Spasms/Stridor).
  • Hypercalcemia = decreased excitability: "Stones (kidney stones), Bones (pain/fractures), Groans (GI: constipation, N/V), Moans (psychosis, confusion)."
  • Hypomagnesemia mimics hypocalcemia and causes it — replace Mg2+ first when both are low.
  • Cancer patients: think hypercalcemia from bone metastases.
  • Pancreatitis, chronic diarrhea, steatorrhea → hypocalcemia (Ca2+ binds to undigested fat).
● Acid-Base — ABG Interpretation NCLEX
  • Normal pH: 7.35–7.45. Below = acidosis; above = alkalosis.
  • Normal PaCO2: 35–45 mm Hg (respiratory component; controlled by lungs).
  • Normal HCO3−: 21–28 mEq/L (metabolic component; controlled by kidneys).
  • ROME: Respiratory = Opposite (pH ↓, PaCO2 ↑ = resp. acidosis); Metabolic = Equal (pH ↓, HCO3− ↓ = met. acidosis).
  • COPD → chronic respiratory acidosis (CO2 retained). Do NOT give high-flow O2 — hypoxic drive concern.
  • Vomiting / NG suction → metabolic alkalosis (losing HCl removes metabolic acid).
  • Diarrhea → metabolic acidosis (losing HCO3−-rich intestinal fluid).
  • DKA → metabolic acidosis with high anion gap (excess ketoacids).
  • Kussmaul breathing (rapid, deep) = compensation for metabolic acidosis.
  • Anxiety/pain/mechanical ventilation → respiratory alkalosis (hyperventilation blows off CO2).
● IV Therapy Safety Rules NCLEX
  • Infiltration: Stop infusion. Elevate extremity. No pressure on site. Warm/cold compress per protocol. Restart IV in opposite extremity.
  • Air embolism: Stop infusion. Position patient on left side, head elevated (traps air in right ventricle away from pulmonary vasculature). Emergency.
  • Phlebitis: Redness + warmth + tenderness along vein = remove the IV; warm compress; document with phlebitis scale.
  • Circulatory overload: Slow or stop IV; elevate HOB; O2; notify provider for diuretic order.
  • Assess peripheral IV sites every 1–4 hours (every 1–2 hours for high-risk patients).
  • D5W is not the same as saline — it acts as a hypotonic solution in the body (water disperses into cells). Does NOT expand ECV effectively.
  • Normal saline (0.9% NaCl) and Lactated Ringer's are first-line for ECV deficit.
  • Rate the infusion before assuming it's running correctly — positional IVs can slow or stop.
● Blood Transfusion Safety NCLEX
  • O-negative = universal donor for packed RBCs. AB-positive = universal recipient.
  • Two nurses verify the blood product before hanging — this is a non-negotiable safety step.
  • Use only 0.9% NS to prime and flush blood tubing — D5W lyses RBCs; LR causes clotting.
  • Acute hemolytic reaction (ABO incompatibility): back/flank pain + hemoglobinuria + hypotension within minutes → STOP transfusion first, then everything else.
  • Stay at bedside for the first 15 minutes — highest reaction risk.
  • Febrile non-hemolytic reaction: fever + chills → stop transfusion; rule out hemolytic before restarting.
  • The nurse is responsible for checking patient ID at the bedside immediately before starting — not just at the blood bank.
● Dehydration vs. ECV Deficit — Know the Difference NCLEX
  • ECV deficit (hypovolemia) = loss of isotonic fluid (Na+ and H2O in equal proportion). Signs: BP/pulse changes, dark urine, weight loss. Normal serum osmolality.
  • Clinical dehydration = ECV deficit plus hypernatremia (more H2O lost than Na+). Adds neurological signs (confusion, seizures). Elevated serum Na+ and osmolality.
  • Common in: infants with gastroenteritis; elderly patients not replacing losses; fever + poor oral intake.
  • Treatment: isotonic fluids first to restore vascular volume, then transition to hypotonic to correct hypernatremia gradually.
● Priority Nursing Actions — Common NCLEX Scenarios NCLEX
  • Patient on furosemide with muscle weakness + U waves on ECG → suspect hypokalemia; hold diuretic; notify provider; check K+ level.
  • Patient with COPD & pH 7.28, PaCO2 60 → respiratory acidosis; priority: improve ventilation (positioning, incentive spirometry, possible intubation).
  • Patient reports tingling around the mouth, positive Chvostek sign → hypocalcemia; check serum Ca2+ and Mg2+.
  • Patient in renal failure gaining weight daily, crackles developing → ECV excess; restrict fluids/Na+; notify provider for diuretic/dialysis orders.
  • Post-op patient with pH 7.50, HCO3− 32 → metabolic alkalosis; check for vomiting, NG suction, or diuretic use; assess K+ level.
  • Patient receiving blood starts complaining of low back pain and chills → Stop the transfusion. Immediately.
  • Confused elderly patient with serum Na+ 152, urine output decreased → hypernatremia; do NOT correct too rapidly; hypotonic fluids as ordered; monitor neuro status.
