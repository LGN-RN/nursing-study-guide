Question 1 of 25
A patient's serum sodium is 160 mEq/L. Which fluid compartment will initially lose the MOST water as the body attempts to equalize osmolality?
Correct Answer: C — Intracellular Hypernatremia (Na >145 mEq/L) creates a hyperosmolar ECF. Water shifts out of cells into the ECF by osmosis to equalize osmolality, causing cells — including brain cells — to shrink. This is why severe hypernatremia causes CNS symptoms (agitation, seizures, coma).
Question 2 of 25
A nurse assesses a patient with severe vomiting and diarrhea for 3 days. Which finding is MOST indicative of fluid volume deficit?
Correct Answer: C — Urine specific gravity of 1.030 In FVD, the kidneys compensate by concentrating urine, raising specific gravity above 1.025 (normal 1.010–1.030). Bounding pulse, hypertension, weight gain, and pitting edema are signs of fluid volume excess (FVE), not deficit.
Question 3 of 25
A patient with chronic heart failure has 3+ pitting edema, dyspnea, and a 5 kg weight gain over 3 days. The nurse anticipates which PRIORITY intervention?
Correct Answer: B — Restrict fluids and administer diuretics FVE (fluid overload) requires reducing excess fluid via restriction and diuretics. A saline bolus would worsen overload. Sodium restriction is indicated (not encouragement). HOB should be elevated 30–45° to ease respiratory effort, not flat.
Question 4 of 25
A patient's serum sodium drops to 115 mEq/L. The nurse observes twitching, confusion, and new-onset seizure activity. Which action is the PRIORITY?
Correct Answer: B — Institute seizure precautions and notify the provider Severe hyponatremia (Na <120 mEq/L) with neurological symptoms is a medical emergency. Patient safety (seizure precautions) comes first, followed by immediate provider notification. Definitive treatment is hypertonic (3%) saline, but that requires a medical order. Free water and oral fluids would worsen dilutional hyponatremia.
Question 5 of 25
A patient has a serum sodium of 158 mEq/L. Which clinical manifestation should the nurse expect?
Correct Answer: C — Intense thirst and dry, sticky mucous membranes Hypernatremia draws water out of cells, causing intense thirst (the primary defense mechanism), dry/sticky mucous membranes, restlessness, and — in severe cases — CNS agitation and seizures. Muscle cramps/tetany = hypocalcemia. Pitting edema = FVE. Paralytic ileus = hypokalemia.
Question 6 of 25
A nurse reviews the ECG of a patient with a serum K+ of 2.8 mEq/L. Which ECG finding is CHARACTERISTIC of this electrolyte imbalance?
Correct Answer: C — Prominent U wave Hypokalemia is uniquely identified by a prominent U wave (a positive deflection after the T wave), along with flattened T waves and ST depression. Tall peaked T waves are the earliest sign of hyperkalemia. Widened QRS and loss of P waves indicate severe hyperkalemia.
Question 7 of 25
A patient's K+ is 6.8 mEq/L with a widened QRS on ECG. The provider orders several treatments. Which medication acts FASTEST to protect the myocardium?
Correct Answer: C — IV calcium gluconate Calcium gluconate stabilizes myocardial cell membranes within 1–3 minutes and is given first to prevent lethal dysrhythmias. It does not lower K+ — it buys time. Insulin + dextrose shifts K+ into cells (onset ~30 min). Kayexalate removes K+ via GI tract (hours). Sodium bicarbonate shifts K+ into cells (30–60 min).
Question 8 of 25
A nurse taps on the facial nerve anterior to the ear of a patient with hypoparathyroidism. The patient's face twitches on that side. The nurse documents this as a positive:
Correct Answer: B — Chvostek's sign Chvostek's sign = unilateral facial muscle twitching elicited by tapping the facial nerve just anterior to the ear. It indicates neuromuscular hyperexcitability from hypocalcemia (or hypomagnesemia). Trousseau's sign = carpal spasm after inflating a BP cuff above systolic for 3 minutes. Homans' = DVT (calf pain on dorsiflexion). Battle's = ecchymosis behind ear from basilar skull fracture.
Question 9 of 25
A patient with acute hypocalcemia develops carpopedal spasm. Which intervention is the PRIORITY?
Correct Answer: B — IV calcium gluconate Acute symptomatic hypocalcemia (carpopedal spasm, laryngospasm, seizures) requires IV calcium gluconate — the fastest route to raise ionized calcium levels. Oral calcium is used for chronic/maintenance management. Vitamin D enhances intestinal absorption but takes days to act. Magnesium may be supplemented concurrently if hypomagnesemia coexists, but IV calcium is the immediate priority.
Question 10 of 25
A patient with metastatic bone cancer has a serum calcium of 13.2 mg/dL. Which symptom pattern should the nurse assess for?
Correct Answer: C — Constipation, polyuria, and bone pain Hypercalcemia is remembered by "Bones, Groans, Stones, Psychic Moans" — bone pain, constipation (GI groans), kidney stones with polyuria/polydipsia, and neuropsychiatric symptoms (depression, confusion). Chvostek's, tetany, hyperreflexia, and laryngospasm are signs of hypocalcemia. Hypercalcemia shortens (not prolongs) the QT interval.
Question 11 of 25
A post-cardiac bypass patient has a magnesium level of 1.2 mEq/L. The nurse anticipates which dangerous dysrhythmia associated with hypomagnesemia?
Correct Answer: B — Torsades de pointes Hypomagnesemia is strongly associated with torsades de pointes — a polymorphic VT that can degenerate into VF. Magnesium is a physiologic calcium antagonist; when depleted, increased neuromuscular and cardiac excitability occurs. IV magnesium sulfate is the treatment of choice for torsades, even when Mg levels are normal.
Question 12 of 25
A patient receiving IV magnesium sulfate for eclampsia has a magnesium level of 9 mEq/L. Which is the EARLIEST sign of magnesium toxicity that the nurse should monitor for?
Correct Answer: C — Loss of deep tendon reflexes (DTRs) Loss of DTRs is the earliest and most important sign of hypermagnesemia toxicity (occurs ~7–10 mEq/L). Respiratory depression follows (~10–13 mEq/L). Cardiac arrest occurs at extremely high levels (≥15 mEq/L). Checking patellar DTR before each dose is a critical nursing action. The antidote is IV calcium gluconate.
Question 13 of 25
A patient with DKA has the following ABGs: pH 7.28, PaCO2 28 mmHg, HCO3- 12 mEq/L. How would the nurse interpret this result?
Correct Answer: C — Metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation Step-by-step: pH 7.28 = acidosis. HCO3- 12 <22 = metabolic origin (primary). PaCO2 28 <35 = lungs blowing off CO2 (compensation). Using ROME: Metabolic = EQUAL (pH↓ and HCO3-↓ both low). The low PaCO2 confirms Kussmaul respirations compensating. pH is still below 7.35 = partially compensated.
Question 14 of 25
A patient with persistent vomiting of gastric contents has: pH 7.52, PaCO2 48 mmHg, HCO3- 36 mEq/L. Which acid-base disorder is occurring?
Correct Answer: C — Metabolic alkalosis with respiratory compensation pH 7.52 = alkalosis. HCO3- 36 >26 = metabolic origin (loss of HCl from vomiting raises bicarbonate). PaCO2 48 >45 = lungs retaining CO2 to compensate. ROME: Metabolic = EQUAL (pH↑ and HCO3-↑ both elevated). pH still above 7.45 = partially compensated metabolic alkalosis.
Question 15 of 25
A COPD patient in acute exacerbation has: pH 7.30, PaCO2 62 mmHg, HCO3- 32 mEq/L. Which interpretation is CORRECT?
Correct Answer: B — Partially compensated respiratory acidosis pH 7.30 = acidosis. PaCO2 62 >45 = respiratory origin (CO2 retention from hypoventilation). HCO3- 32 >26 = kidneys retaining bicarbonate to compensate. ROME: Respiratory = OPPOSITE (pH↓, PaCO2↑). Since pH is STILL below 7.35, compensation is present but incomplete = partially compensated. Fully compensated would show pH within 7.35–7.45.
Question 16 of 25
A patient having a panic attack is hyperventilating. ABGs: pH 7.52, PaCO2 28 mmHg, HCO3- 24 mEq/L. Which intervention addresses the PRIMARY cause?
Correct Answer: B — Coach slow, deep breathing Hyperventilation blows off CO2 → respiratory alkalosis. The primary treatment is to slow the respiratory rate so CO2 accumulates, lowering pH back to normal. HCO3- 24 is normal (no metabolic component). Sodium bicarbonate would worsen alkalosis. High-flow O2 does not correct the problem. Intubation is not indicated for anxiety-induced hyperventilation.
Question 17 of 25
A patient is admitted with severe dehydration and hypotension after running a marathon in heat. The nurse anticipates which IV fluid to expand intravascular volume?
Correct Answer: D — 0.9% NaCl (normal saline) Isotonic 0.9% NaCl stays in the ECF and expands intravascular volume — ideal for volume resuscitation. D5W is effectively hypotonic after dextrose is metabolized; it distributes throughout all compartments and minimally expands intravascular volume. 0.45% NaCl is hypotonic and shifts water into cells, worsening intravascular depletion. 3% NaCl is reserved for severe symptomatic hyponatremia.
Question 18 of 25
A patient receives 3% NaCl for severe symptomatic hyponatremia. The nurse knows that correcting sodium too rapidly can cause:
Correct Answer: B — Osmotic demyelination syndrome Brain cells chronically adapt to low osmolality by expelling solutes. Rapid correction raises ECF osmolality quickly, drawing water out of brain cells and causing irreversible demyelination of pontine neurons. Sodium should be corrected no faster than 10–12 mEq/L per 24 hours. Symptoms of ODS include dysarthria, dysphagia, seizures, and locked-in syndrome.
Question 19 of 25
A patient with SIADH has a urine osmolality of 800 mOsm/kg and serum osmolality of 260 mOsm/kg. The nurse expects which intervention to be ordered FIRST?
Correct Answer: B — Fluid restriction to 800–1,000 mL/day SIADH causes excess ADH → water retention → dilutional hyponatremia. Highly concentrated urine with dilute serum is the hallmark. The primary treatment is fluid restriction to allow the body to gradually excrete retained water and correct hyponatremia. DDAVP is ADH — it would worsen SIADH. IV saline adds volume. Furosemide may be used adjunctively in severe cases but is not the first-line treatment.
Question 20 of 25
A patient undergoing aggressive refeeding after prolonged starvation develops muscle weakness, confusion, and paresthesias. Which electrolyte imbalance is the MOST likely cause?
Correct Answer: B — Hypophosphatemia Refeeding syndrome causes hypophosphatemia — glucose infusion triggers insulin release, which drives phosphate into cells. Phosphate depletion impairs ATP and 2,3-DPG production in RBCs (reducing oxygen delivery). Clinical effects include muscle weakness (including the diaphragm → respiratory failure risk), confusion, and paresthesias. Severe hypophosphatemia can also cause hemolytic anemia.
Question 21 of 25
A patient with end-stage renal disease has pH 7.25, HCO3- 10 mEq/L, and is breathing rapidly and deeply. The nurse recognizes this breathing pattern as:
Correct Answer: C — Kussmaul respirations Kussmaul respirations — deep, labored, rapid breathing without periods of apnea — are the body's respiratory compensation for metabolic acidosis. The lungs blow off CO2 to raise pH. This pattern is unique to metabolic acidosis and is commonly seen in DKA and renal failure. Cheyne-Stokes = crescendo-decrescendo with apneic episodes (CNS dysfunction). Biot's = irregular breathing with apnea (brainstem damage).
Question 22 of 25
A patient with cirrhosis has serum albumin of 1.8 g/dL and massive ascites. The nurse understands edema develops because of:
Correct Answer: B — Decreased plasma oncotic pressure Albumin is the major protein that creates plasma oncotic (colloid osmotic) pressure, which holds water inside blood vessels. When albumin is critically low (as in liver failure), plasma oncotic pressure falls → fluid leaks out of capillaries into interstitium and body cavities → edema and ascites. This is a Starling forces imbalance. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure (heart failure, venous obstruction) is a separate mechanism.
Question 23 of 25
The nurse is preparing to administer IV potassium chloride to a patient with K+ of 2.9 mEq/L. Which action is CORRECT?
Correct Answer: B — Dilute and infuse via pump at ≤10 mEq/hr peripheral IV potassium is NEVER given as an IV push (risk of fatal cardiac arrest). It must always be diluted and administered via infusion pump. Maximum safe rate: 10 mEq/hr peripheral (20 mEq/hr central with continuous cardiac monitoring). Potassium is never given IM (causes severe tissue necrosis and pain). Monitor the IV site for phlebitis during infusion.
Question 24 of 25
A patient with a PICC line develops redness, warmth, and swelling at the insertion site along with a fever of 38.9°C. Which action is the PRIORITY?
Correct Answer: C — Stop the infusion and notify the provider Signs of CVAD-related infection (local inflammation + systemic fever) indicate a potential catheter-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI). The priority is to stop the infusion and notify the provider immediately. The provider will order blood cultures (from both the catheter and a peripheral site) and likely removal of the catheter. Continuing to infuse through a potentially infected catheter risks sepsis and bacteremia.
Question 25 of 25
A patient receiving multiple blood transfusions and experiencing persistent vomiting has: pH 7.50, PaCO2 50 mmHg, HCO3- 38 mEq/L. Which acid-base disorder is present?
Correct Answer: B — Metabolic alkalosis with respiratory compensation pH 7.50 = alkalosis. HCO3- 38 >26 = metabolic origin (vomiting removes HCl; blood transfusion citrate is metabolized to HCO3-). PaCO2 50 >45 = lungs retaining CO2 to compensate. ROME: Metabolic = EQUAL (pH↑ and HCO3-↑ both elevated). pH still above 7.45 = partially compensated. If PaCO2 were normal (35–45), it would be uncompensated.
