1 — Body Fluid Compartments & Electrolytes

Learning Outcomes

  • Describe the composition of the major body fluid compartments.
  • Define electrolytes and their roles in homeostasis.
  • Identify normal serum electrolyte reference values.

Body Water Content

Water accounts for 50–60% of adult body weight. Fat tissue has a lower percentage of water, so individuals with more body fat have a lower total water content. Women and older adults tend to have less lean body mass and therefore lower body water percentages. Older adults average 45–50% body water — placing them at higher risk for fluid-related problems.

1 kg (2.2 lb) weight change = 1 L of fluid gained or lost. Daily weight is the most accurate measure of volume status.

Electrolytes

Electrolytes are substances that dissociate into ions when in solution. Cations (positive): Na+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+. Anions (negative): HCO3-, Cl-, PO43-. Concentration is expressed in milliequivalents (mEq) per liter.

Electrolyte Distribution

  • ECF main cation: Na+ (also small amounts of K+, Ca2+, Mg2+). Main anion: Cl-
  • ICF main cation: K+ (also small Mg2+, Na+). Main anion: PO43-

2 — Fluid Movement & Osmolality

Mechanisms of Fluid Movement

Diffusion
Movement from high to low concentration; requires no energy; stops when concentrations equalize.
Facilitated Diffusion
Uses a protein carrier to move large molecules (e.g., glucose) across a membrane; passive, no energy required.
Active Transport
Moves molecules against concentration gradient; requires ATP. Example: sodium-potassium pump — pumps Na+ out and K+ into cells.
Osmosis
Water moves from low-solute (high water) to high-solute (low water) area across a semipermeable membrane; requires no energy.

Osmolality

Osmolality measures concentration of molecules per kilogram of water (mOsm/kg). It is the preferred measure for plasma, urine, and other body fluids.

Plasma Osmolality Formula Plasma osmolality = (2 × Na+) + (BUN ÷ 2.8) + (Glucose ÷ 18)
Normal: 280–295 mOsm/kg
>295 = water deficit (concentrated) | <275 = water excess (dilute)

Capillary Fluid Exchange

Four pressures govern fluid movement between capillaries and interstitial space:

  • Capillary hydrostatic pressure — pushes fluid OUT of capillaries (↑ at arterial end ~40 mmHg)
  • Plasma oncotic pressure — pulls fluid INTO capillaries (~25 mmHg; driven by albumin)
  • Interstitial hydrostatic pressure — pushes fluid back into capillaries (~1 mmHg)
  • Interstitial oncotic pressure — draws fluid out (~1 mmHg)

At the arterial end: hydrostatic pressure exceeds oncotic → fluid leaves capillary.
At the venous end: oncotic pressure dominates → fluid returns to capillary.

Fig. 17.8 — Starling forces: capillary fluid exchange (Lewis p. 306)

Edema Formation

  • ↑ Venous hydrostatic pressure (heart failure, venous thrombosis)
  • ↓ Plasma oncotic pressure (hypoalbuminemia from liver disease, malnutrition, nephrotic syndrome)
  • ↑ Interstitial oncotic pressure (burns, trauma, inflammation — proteins leak into interstitium)
  • Lymphatic obstruction

Fluid Spacing

First spacing
Normal distribution in ICF and ECF.
Second spacing
Abnormal accumulation of interstitial fluid (edema).
Third spacing
Fluid trapped in a nonfunctional area (ascites, peritonitis, pancreatitis, burns). This fluid cannot move back — causes effective hypovolemia despite total body fluid excess.

Thirst is the primary protection against hyperosmolality. Patients who are unconscious, cognitively impaired, or unable to swallow cannot respond to thirst — highest risk for hypernatremia.
3 — Fluid Volume Imbalances (FVD & FVE)
Fluid volume deficit (FVD) ≠ dehydration. Dehydration = pure water loss without sodium loss. FVD = loss of both water and sodium-containing fluid.

Nursing Management: Fluid Imbalances

  • Daily weights — same time, same scale, same clothes; 1 kg ↑ = 1 L fluid retained
  • I&O — include all sources (oral, IV, tube feeds, wound drainage, urine, emesis, diaphoresis)
  • Urine SG — >1.025 = concentrated (FVD); <1.010 = dilute (FVE)
  • Orthostatic BP — check for orthostatic hypotension (FVD indicator)
  • Skin turgor — assess over sternum, abdomen, anterior forearm (not reliable in older adults)
Fig. 17.11 — Skin turgor: normal rebound (A) vs. poor turgor/tenting in FVD (B, C) (Lewis p. 310)
  • Safety — fall precautions for orthostatic hypotension and confusion; seizure precautions if severe
  • Lung sounds — crackles suggest FVE/pulmonary edema
  • Edema grading: 1+ = 2-mm indentation; 4+ = 8-mm indentation (pitting edema)
Nursing Priority For FVD: encourage oral fluids; give IV fluids per order; monitor rate carefully (especially in cardiac/renal/neurologic patients).
For FVE: elevate HOB for dyspnea; restrict fluids and sodium; monitor for pulmonary edema; weigh daily.
4 — Sodium Imbalances

Na+ (normal 136–145 mEq/L) is the main ECF cation and the primary determinant of ECF osmolality. It regulates water distribution, nerve impulse generation, muscle contractility, and acid-base balance.

Serum sodium reflects the ratio of sodium to water — not just the total amount of sodium. Changes in Na+ = parallel changes in osmolality.

  • Neurologic: drowsiness, restlessness, confusion, lethargy → seizures, coma
  • If ECF deficit: postural hypotension, tachycardia, weight loss
  • If ECF excess: edema, weight gain, ↑ BP
  • Neurologic (CNS first): headache, irritability, difficulty concentrating
  • Severe: confusion, vomiting, seizures, coma
  • Rapid severe drop → irreversible brain herniation

  • If ECF excess: weight gain, edema, ↑ BP

5 — Potassium Imbalances

K+ (normal 3.5–5.0 mEq/L) is the major ICF cation — 98% of body potassium is inside cells (~140 mEq/L intracellular vs. 3.5–5.0 mEq/L ECF). The ECF-to-ICF ratio of K+ determines resting membrane potential of nerve and muscle cells. Small changes in serum K+ have major effects on cardiac conduction.

Insulin stimulates the Na+-K+ pump, driving K+ into cells. Acidosis drives K+ OUT of cells (H+ enters cells in exchange → hyperkalemia). Alkalosis drives K+ INTO cells → hypokalemia.

6 — Calcium Imbalances

Ca2+ (normal total 9.0–10.5 mg/dL; ionized 4.5–5.6 mg/dL) is the primary cation in bone and teeth. It is essential for blood clotting, nerve impulse transmission, myocardial contraction, and muscle contraction. 99% of calcium is in bone; 1% in plasma and cells.

Important Calcium Facts

  • Of plasma calcium: ~50% bound to albumin, ~40% ionized (biologically active), ~10% bound to phosphate/citrate/carbonate.
  • pH affects ionized calcium: Acidosis → ↑ ionized Ca2+ (less binding to albumin). Alkalosis → ↓ ionized Ca2+ (more binding to albumin → symptomatic hypocalcemia even with normal total Ca2+).
  • Always check albumin when interpreting total calcium. Low albumin → falsely low total calcium despite normal ionized.
  • PTH → ↑ Ca2+ (bone resorption, GI absorption, renal reabsorption). Calcitonin (thyroid) → ↓ Ca2+ (opposite of PTH).
  • Reciprocal relationship: ↑ Ca2+ = ↓ PO43-, and vice versa.

7 — Phosphate & Magnesium Imbalances

Phosphate (PO43-) — Normal: 3.0–4.5 mg/dL

Phosphorus is the primary ICF anion and second most abundant element after calcium. It is essential for muscle, RBC, and nervous system function; involved in ATP synthesis, acid-base buffering, and carbohydrate/protein/fat metabolism. Regulated by PTH and kidneys. Reciprocal relationship with Ca2+: ↑ PO43- = ↓ Ca2+.

Hyperphosphatemia (PO43- >4.5 mg/dL)Hypophosphatemia (PO43- <3.0 mg/dL)
Main Causes Renal failure (most common), phosphate-containing laxatives/enemas (Fleet Enema), hypoparathyroidism, vitamin D intoxication, tumor lysis syndrome, rhabdomyolysis Malabsorption, diarrhea, phosphate-binding antacids, refeeding syndrome, DKA treatment, respiratory alkalosis, malnutrition, parenteral nutrition without adequate phosphate
Manifestations Usually asymptomatic unless Ca2+ binds phosphate → signs of hypocalcemia (tetany, muscle cramps, paresthesias, hypotension, seizures). Long-term: calcified deposits in soft tissues, joints, arteries, corneas, kidneys → organ dysfunction Impaired cellular energy (↓ ATP, ↓ 2,3-DPG) → ↓ O2 delivery. Mild: asymptomatic. Severe: CNS depression, muscle weakness, respiratory failure, heart failure. Chronic: rickets, osteomalacia.
Treatment Restrict phosphate-rich foods (dairy), phosphate-binding agents (calcium carbonate), hemodialysis for severe cases, correct hypocalcemia Mild: oral phosphate supplements or dairy products. Severe: IV sodium phosphate or potassium phosphate (monitor Ca2+, PO43- every 6–12 h)

Magnesium (Mg2+) — Normal: 1.3–2.1 mEq/L

Mg2+ is the second most abundant ICF cation. It is a cofactor in many enzyme systems (carbohydrate metabolism, DNA/protein synthesis, blood glucose, BP regulation). It powers the Na+-K+ pump (ATP production). Low Mg2+ impairs K+ replacement — always check and correct Mg2+ when treating hypokalemia. Stored mainly in muscle and bone (50–60%); only 1% in ECF.

Hypermagnesemia (Mg2+ >2.1 mEq/L)Hypomagnesemia (Mg2+ <1.3 mEq/L)

  • Always administer via infusion pump
  • Monitor VS, LOC, and deep tendon reflexes (DTRs) frequently — loss of DTRs is earliest sign of toxicity

  • Keep IV calcium gluconate at bedside as antidote
Hypomagnesemia mimics and worsens hypocalcemia AND hypokalemia. When K+ fails to correct despite replacement, always check Mg2+ — low Mg2+ causes renal K+ wasting via aldosterone stimulation.
8 — Acid-Base Balance & Regulation

pH Fundamentals

pH is the negative logarithm of H+ concentration. Lower pH = higher H+ = more acidic. Normal arterial blood pH = 7.35–7.45.

  • pH <7.35 = acidosis
  • pH >7.45 = alkalosis
  • pH <6.8 or >7.8 = incompatible with life
Fig. 17.16 — pH scale: acidosis / normal 7.35–7.45 / alkalosis / lethal limits (Lewis p. 321)
The body maintains a 20:1 ratio of HCO3- to carbonic acid (H2CO3). This 20:1 ratio = pH 7.40. Compensation maintains this ratio even when absolute values change.

Three Regulatory Mechanisms

SystemSpeedMechanism
Buffer System (chemical)Seconds — fastestConverts strong acids to weaker ones; maintains pH until lungs/kidneys act. Main buffer: HCO3-/H2CO3. Others: phosphate, protein, hemoglobin. Cannot eliminate H+ — only minimizes change.
Respiratory SystemMinutes (effective in hours)Lungs excrete CO2 + H2O. CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3-. ↑ RR/depth (hyperventilation) → blow off CO2 → ↓ H+ → ↑ pH. ↓ RR (hypoventilation) → retain CO2 → ↑ H+ → ↓ pH.
Renal SystemHours to days — sustained correctionKidneys excrete H+ and regulate HCO3- reabsorption. In acidosis: retain HCO3-, excrete H+ (urine pH ↓ to 4). In alkalosis: excrete HCO3- (urine pH ↑ to 8). Normal urine pH = 6. Most powerful but slowest.

Potassium-H+ Relationship

  • Acidosis: H+ enters cells → K+ shifts OUT → hyperkalemia
  • Alkalosis: H+ leaves cells → K+ shifts IN → hypokalemia

The Carbonic Acid-Bicarbonate Buffer

HCl (strong acid) + NaHCO3 (strong base) → NaCl (salt) + H2CO3 (weak acid) → CO2 + H2O (excreted by lungs)

Compensation vs. Correction

Compensation
The secondary system (opposite from the primary disorder) works to return the pH toward normal. The ratio remains 20:1 but may not reach exactly 7.40.
Partial compensation
Compensating value is abnormal but pH remains outside normal range.
Full compensation
pH returns to within normal range (7.35–7.45) though PaCO2 and HCO3- are still abnormal.
Correction
Both pH and the underlying cause return to normal. The body itself cannot always achieve correction.
9 — ABG Interpretation

Normal ABG Values

5-Step ABG Interpretation Method

  1. Step 1 — Look at all values. If all are within normal range → ABGs are normal. If any are abnormal → continue.
  2. Step 2 — Assess the pH. pH <7.35 = acidosis; pH >7.45 = alkalosis. Normal pH = normal, compensated, or mixed disorder.
  3. Step 3 — Use ROME to determine origin.
  4. Step 4 — Assess compensation. Look at the remaining value (CO2 if metabolic; HCO3- if respiratory). If moving in opposite direction → compensating. If pH still abnormal → partial compensation. If pH normal → full compensation.
  5. Step 5 — Assess PaO2 and SaO2. Values below normal = hypoxemia.
🔑 Mnemonic — ABG Interpretation Step 3
ROME: Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal
Respiratory — OPPOSITE directions
  • Resp. Acidosis: pH↓ & PaCO2↑ (pH down, CO2 up)
  • Resp. Alkalosis: pH↑ & PaCO2↓ (pH up, CO2 down)
Metabolic — EQUAL (same) direction
  • Met. Acidosis: pH↓ & HCO3−↓ (both down)
  • Met. Alkalosis: pH↑ & HCO3−↑ (both up)

Mixed Acid-Base Disorders

Two or more primary imbalances occur simultaneously. pH depends on type, severity, and compensation.

  • Resp. acidosis + metabolic alkalosis (e.g., COPD + NG suction) → near-normal pH

  • Mixed alkalosis (e.g., postop pain → hyperventilation + NG suction) → high pH

13 — Common Related Diseases & Pharmacology

SIADH — Syndrome of Inappropriate ADH Secretion

Pathophysiology

Excess ADH → excess free water retention → dilutional hyponatremia. Causes: CNS disorders (stroke, meningitis, head injury), pulmonary disease (pneumonia, TB), drugs (SSRIs, carbamazepine, cyclophosphamide, NSAIDs), major surgery.

Key Signs & Symptoms

  • Hyponatremia (Na+ <136 mEq/L), low serum osmolality (<275 mOsm/kg)
  • High urine sodium (>20 mEq/L) + concentrated urine despite low serum osmolality — the diagnostic paradox of SIADH
  • Neurologic: headache, confusion, irritability, seizures, coma (cerebral edema from water shift into brain)
  • Weight gain without edema — water retained intracellularly; Na+ is normal or low, not high

Pharmacology

  • Fluid restriction — 800–1000 mL/day; primary treatment
  • Tolvaptan (Samsca) — oral vasopressin V2 antagonist ("vaptan"); promotes free water excretion; for moderate/severe SIADH
  • Conivaptan (Vaprisol) — IV vasopressin V1a/V2 antagonist; inpatient use
  • 3% NaCl (hypertonic saline) — only for severe symptomatic hyponatremia (seizures, coma); correct ≤10–12 mEq/L in first 24 h (osmotic demyelination risk)
  • Loop diuretics (furosemide) — promote free water excretion; often used with saline
Diabetes Insipidus (DI) — Central

Pathophysiology

ADH deficiency (central DI) or ADH resistance (nephrogenic DI). Central causes: head trauma, neurosurgery, brain tumors, CNS infections. Result: inability to concentrate urine → massive dilute urine output → hypernatremia.

Key Signs & Symptoms

  • Massive polyuria — 3–20 L/day of very dilute urine (urine SG <1.005, urine osmolality <200 mOsm/kg)
  • Intense polydipsia (compensatory drinking)
  • Hypernatremia (>145 mEq/L), ↑ serum osmolality
  • Dehydration, hypotension, tachycardia if unable to replace losses

Pharmacology

  • Desmopressin (DDAVP) — synthetic ADH; first-line for central DI; intranasal, oral, or IV/SQ
  • Vasopressin (Pitressin) — natural ADH; short-acting; used acutely/postoperatively
  • Hypotonic fluids (0.45% NaCl or D5W) — replace free water deficit
  • Nephrogenic DI: thiazide diuretics + low-Na+ diet (paradoxically ↓ urine output); NSAIDs
DKA — Diabetic Ketoacidosis

Pathophysiology

Insulin deficiency → cells cannot use glucose → fat breakdown → ketone bodies → high anion gap metabolic acidosis. Hyperglycemia → osmotic diuresis → fluid and electrolyte (K+, Na+, PO43-) losses. Triggers: new-onset T1DM, missed insulin, infection, physiologic stress.

Key Signs & Symptoms

  • Hyperglycemia (>250 mg/dL), pH <7.35, HCO3- <15, high anion gap (>12)
  • Kussmaul respirations — deep, labored, rapid breathing; compensatory for metabolic acidosis (blowing off CO2)
  • Fruity/acetone breath — ketone exhalation; specific to DKA/starvation ketosis
  • Polydipsia, polyuria, weight loss, dehydration, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain
  • Hyperkalemia on labs (K+ shifts out of cells) — but total body K+ is depleted
  • Confusion, lethargy, coma if severe

Pharmacology

  • IV Regular Insulin — continuous infusion; lowers glucose and reverses ketosis; never use rapid-acting analogs IV
  • IV 0.9% NaCl — first fluid bolus (1–2 L); switch to 0.45% NaCl when Na+ normalizes
  • Potassium replacement (KCl) — critical; check K+ before starting insulin; replace if K+ <5.5 before insulin (insulin drives K+ into cells → hypokalemia)
  • Dextrose (D5 or D10) — add to IV when glucose reaches 200–250 mg/dL to prevent hypoglycemia while continuing insulin drip
  • NaHCO3 — only for severe acidosis (pH <7.0); rarely needed
Heart Failure — Fluid Volume Excess (FVE)

Pathophysiology

Reduced cardiac output → RAAS + ADH activation → Na+ and water retention → systemic and pulmonary congestion. Left-sided HF: pulmonary edema, dyspnea. Right-sided HF: peripheral edema, JVD, hepatomegaly.

Key Signs & Symptoms

  • Dyspnea, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea
  • Crackles (rales), pulmonary edema, frothy pink sputum (severe)
  • Pitting edema (ankles/sacrum), weight gain, bounding pulse, ↑ CVP/JVD
  • S3 heart sound (ventricular gallop) — pathognomonic for volume overload/heart failure; indicates the ventricle is overfilled
  • Dilutional hyponatremia, hypokalemia (from diuretics)

Pharmacology

  • Furosemide (Lasix) — loop diuretic; first-line for fluid removal; monitor K+, Na+, BUN/Cr; ototoxicity with rapid IV push
  • ACE inhibitors (lisinopril, enalapril) — reduce preload/afterload; block RAAS; mainstay of chronic HF
  • Beta-blockers (carvedilol, metoprolol succinate) — reduce workload; improve survival; start low and titrate slowly
  • Spironolactone (Aldactone) — K+-sparing diuretic / aldosterone antagonist; improves mortality; monitor K+
  • Digoxin — positive inotrope; narrow therapeutic window; toxic at low K+ → always monitor K+
  • Sodium & fluid restriction — <2 g Na/day; fluid restriction for hyponatremia
Hypoparathyroidism — Hypocalcemia

Pathophysiology

Low or absent PTH → ↓ bone Ca2+ resorption, ↓ renal Ca2+ reabsorption, ↓ GI absorption → hypocalcemia → ↑ neuromuscular excitability. Most common cause: inadvertent removal or trauma to parathyroid glands during thyroid/neck surgery.

Key Signs & Symptoms

  • Muscle cramps, tetany, laryngeal stridor, seizures
  • Numbness/tingling: circumoral, fingertips, toes
  • Chvostek sign — facial muscle twitch when tapping facial nerve; specific to hypocalcemia and hypomagnesemia
  • Trousseau sign — carpal spasm when BP cuff inflated above systolic × 3 min; more specific than Chvostek
  • Prolonged QT interval → risk of torsades de pointes
  • Anxiety, depression, irritability

Pharmacology

  • IV Calcium gluconate — acute/symptomatic; give slowly (rapid → hypotension, bradycardia); keep at bedside post-thyroid/parathyroid surgery
  • Oral calcium carbonate or citrate — chronic replacement; carbonate with meals; citrate between meals (better absorbed)
  • Calcitriol (active Vitamin D) — needed when PTH absent to enhance GI Ca2+ absorption
  • Magnesium replacement — if hypomagnesemia contributing; low Mg2+ impairs PTH secretion and release
Primary Hyperparathyroidism — Hypercalcemia

Pathophysiology

Excess PTH (usually from parathyroid adenoma) → ↑ bone resorption, ↑ renal Ca2+ reabsorption, ↑ GI absorption → hypercalcemia. Second most common cause of hypercalcemia (malignancy is #1).

Key Signs & Symptoms

  • "Bones, Groans, Stones, Psychic Moans" — the mnemonic that encompasses the classic presentation of hypercalcemia
  • Bone pain, fractures (osteoclastic resorption)
  • Kidney stones, polyuria, polydipsia
  • N/V, constipation, anorexia (GI "groans")
  • Fatigue, confusion, depression, psychosis
  • Short QT interval; ↑ sensitivity to digoxin toxicity

Pharmacology

  • IV 0.9% NaCl (volume expansion) — first step; dilutes Ca2+ and promotes renal excretion
  • Furosemide (loop diuretic) — after hydration; promotes calciuresis (Ca2+ excretion)
  • Zoledronic acid (Zometa) / Pamidronate — bisphosphonates; gold standard; onset 2–4 days; inhibit osteoclasts
  • Calcitonin (Miacalcin) — rapid onset (hours) but tachyphylaxis within 48 h; short-term bridge
  • Denosumab (Xgeva) — RANK-L inhibitor; for bisphosphonate-refractory cases
  • Dialysis — life-threatening or renal failure cases
Addison's Disease — Primary Adrenal Insufficiency

Pathophysiology

Destruction of adrenal cortex (autoimmune most common; also TB, fungal, hemorrhage) → deficiency of both cortisol and aldosterone. Without aldosterone: Na+ wasting → hyponatremia; K+ retention → hyperkalemia; volume depletion → hypotension.

Key Signs & Symptoms

  • Hyperpigmentation — bronzing/darkening of skin, especially sun-exposed areas, knuckles, buccal mucosa; caused by excess ACTH stimulating melanocytes; unique to primary adrenal insufficiency (not secondary)
  • Hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia
  • Hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, weakness, profound fatigue
  • N/V, weight loss, anorexia, salt craving
  • Addisonian crisis: severe hypotension, vomiting, shock — life-threatening (triggered by stress, infection, surgery)

Pharmacology

  • Hydrocortisone (Solu-Cortef) — glucocorticoid replacement; chronic: oral hydrocortisone or prednisone; crisis: IV hydrocortisone 100 mg bolus, then continuous infusion
  • Fludrocortisone (Florinef) — mineralocorticoid replacement; oral daily; restores Na+/K+ balance and blood pressure
  • IV 0.9% NaCl + Dextrose — adrenal crisis: rapid volume expansion + glucose correction
  • Stress dosing rule — patients must double/triple steroid dose during illness, surgery, or physical stress to prevent crisis
Cushing's Syndrome — Glucocorticoid Excess

Pathophysiology

Excess cortisol — endogenous (pituitary adenoma = Cushing's disease; adrenal tumor; ectopic ACTH) or exogenous (long-term steroid therapy, most common). Cortisol promotes Na+ retention, K+ excretion, gluconeogenesis → hyperglycemia.

Key Signs & Symptoms

  • Buffalo hump (dorsocervical fat pad) + moon face + central obesity + purple/violaceous striae — characteristic cluster unique to Cushing's syndrome
  • Hypokalemia, mild hypernatremia, metabolic alkalosis
  • Hypertension, hyperglycemia, osteoporosis
  • Proximal muscle weakness, thin/fragile skin, easy bruising
  • Immunosuppression → frequent infections

Pharmacology

  • Treat underlying cause — surgical resection for pituitary or adrenal tumor (transsphenoidal surgery for Cushing's disease)
  • Taper steroids — for iatrogenic (medication-induced) Cushing's; never stop abruptly (risk of adrenal crisis)
  • Ketoconazole / Metyrapone / Mitotane — adrenal steroidogenesis inhibitors; used when surgery not feasible
  • Mifepristone (Korlym) — glucocorticoid receptor antagonist; for Cushing's with hyperglycemia
  • KCl replacement — correct hypokalemia; antihypertensives for cortisol-induced HTN
Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) / Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

Pathophysiology

Reduced GFR → impaired excretion of K+, phosphate, and H+ → hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, metabolic acidosis. Fluid retention → FVE. Reduced erythropoietin → anemia. Reduced vitamin D activation → hypocalcemia → secondary hyperparathyroidism.

Key Signs & Symptoms

  • Oliguria/anuria — hallmark of AKI; urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hr signals acute loss of renal function
  • Hyperkalemia (peaked T waves, VF risk), hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia
  • Metabolic acidosis → Kussmaul respirations if severe
  • FVE: hypertension, edema, pulmonary edema
  • Uremic frost — white crystalline urea deposits on skin; a late/severe sign seen only in uremia
  • Asterixis (flapping tremor), confusion, uremic encephalopathy

Pharmacology

  • Furosemide (loop diuretic) — fluid management when kidneys still producing urine
  • Patiromer (Veltassa) / Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (Lokelma) — oral K+ binders; preferred over Kayexalate
  • Kayexalate (sodium polystyrene sulfonate) — older K+ binder; risk of intestinal necrosis; less used
  • Calcium carbonate / Sevelamer — phosphate binders; take with meals
  • Sodium bicarbonate — for metabolic acidosis; goal HCO3- >22 mEq/L
  • Erythropoietin (EPO) / Darbepoetin — renal anemia; always give with iron supplementation
  • Calcitriol (active Vitamin D) — secondary hyperparathyroidism prevention/treatment
  • Dialysis — ESRD or refractory AKI; immediate indication: fluid overload, uremia, hyperkalemia, acidosis (AEIOU mnemonic)
COPD — Chronic Respiratory Acidosis

Pathophysiology

Airflow obstruction (emphysema + chronic bronchitis) → air trapping → CO2 retention → chronic respiratory acidosis. Kidneys compensate by retaining HCO3- → compensated respiratory acidosis. COPD patients depend on hypoxic drive — high O2 can suppress breathing and worsen CO2 retention.

Key Signs & Symptoms

  • Chronic productive cough, wheezing, dyspnea on exertion
  • Barrel chest (increased A-P diameter) — from chronic air trapping and hyperinflation; specific to emphysema/severe COPD
  • Pursed-lip breathing — self-taught PEEP technique to slow exhalation; seen specifically in emphysema
  • ABG: pH low-normal (7.35–7.38 compensated), PaCO2 >45, HCO3- elevated (renal compensation)
  • Polycythemia (↑ RBCs from chronic hypoxia), cyanosis, clubbing (late)

Pharmacology

  • Albuterol (SABA) — rescue bronchodilator; short-acting β2 agonist; rapid bronchodilation
  • Tiotropium (Spiriva) — LAMA; long-acting anticholinergic; maintenance; reduces exacerbations
  • Inhaled corticosteroids (fluticasone) — for frequent exacerbators; reduce airway inflammation
  • Supplemental O2 — titrate to SpO2 88–92%; 1–2 L/min only to avoid suppressing hypoxic drive → CO2 narcosis
  • Systemic corticosteroids (prednisone) — short course (5 days) for acute exacerbations
  • Antibiotics — for bacterial exacerbations (azithromycin, doxycycline)
  • BiPAP / mechanical ventilation — for respiratory failure; BiPAP preferred non-invasively
14 — NCLEX Critical To Knows
1 — Fluid Volume Assessment Priorities HIGH YIELD
  • Daily weight is the most accurate measure of fluid status (1 kg = 1 L)
  • Orthostatic hypotension (BP drops ≥20 mmHg systolic / ≥10 mmHg diastolic when moving from lying to standing) = significant FVD
  • FVE signs: bounding pulse, JVD, S3, crackles, pitting edema, weight gain
  • Urine SG >1.025 = concentrated (FVD); <1.010 = dilute (FVE)
  • Older adults: most reliable site for skin turgor is the sternum or abdomen (not forearm/back of hand — skin naturally less elastic)
2 — Potassium Safety Rules HIGH YIELD
  • NEVER give IV KCl as a bolus or IV push — fatal cardiac arrest
  • Max peripheral rate: 10 mEq/hr; may go faster in ICU with continuous ECG + central line
  • Give KCl only if urine output ≥ 0.5 mL/kg/hr
  • Low K+ + digoxin = high toxicity risk; monitor for N/V, vision changes, yellow/halo vision, bradycardia
  • Always correct hypomagnesemia before/alongside hypokalemia — hypomagnesemia causes renal K+ wasting
  • ECG changes in hyperkalemia (in order): peaked T waves → loss of P waves → widened QRS → ventricular fibrillation
  • IV calcium gluconate for hyperkalemia = stabilizes heart; does NOT lower K+
3 — Calcium Tetany Signs HIGH YIELD
  • Chvostek sign: Tap on facial nerve in front of ear → facial muscle twitching = hypocalcemia
  • Trousseau sign: Inflate BP cuff above systolic for 3 min → carpal (hand/wrist) spasm = hypocalcemia
  • Both signs are also positive in hypomagnesemia
  • After thyroid/parathyroid/neck surgery: monitor closely for hypocalcemia (circumoral tingling, positive Chvostek/Trousseau)
  • Alkalosis → ↑ albumin binding → ↓ ionized Ca2+ → tetany even with normal total Ca2+
  • Hypercalcemia mnemonic: Bones, Groans, Stones, Psychic Moans
4 — Sodium Correction Rate Rules HIGH YIELD
  • Hypernatremia: Correct no faster than 8–15 mEq/L per 8 hours → too-rapid correction → cerebral edema (water rushes back into brain cells)
  • Hyponatremia: Correct no faster than 10–12 mEq/L in first 24 h and ≤18 mEq/L in 48 h → too-rapid correction → osmotic demyelination syndrome (permanent myelin sheath damage)
  • Always initiate seizure precautions for patients with significant sodium imbalances or altered consciousness
  • SIADH → dilutional hyponatremia → treat with fluid restriction; vasopressin antagonists (conivaptan, tolvaptan) for severe/refractory cases
5 — ABG Interpretation: ROME HIGH YIELD
  • ROME: Respiratory = Opposite (pH and PaCO2 move in opposite directions). Metabolic = Equal (pH and HCO3- move in same direction).
  • Kussmaul respirations (deep, rapid) = body's respiratory compensation for metabolic acidosis
  • Primary respiratory disorder → kidneys compensate (takes hours to days)
  • Primary metabolic disorder → lungs compensate (takes minutes to hours)
  • Anion gap: Na+ − (HCO3- + Cl-) = normal 8–12. High anion gap = acid gain (DKA, lactic acidosis). Normal anion gap = HCO3- loss (diarrhea).
  • Alkalosis (both types) → watch for hypokalemia and hypocalcemia (tetany, dysrhythmias)
6 — IV Fluid Selection Rules HIGH YIELD
  • 0.9% NS (normal saline) = only IV solution compatible with blood/blood products
  • LR = best for surgery, burns, GI losses (closest to ECF); avoid in liver failure, hyperkalemia, lactic acidosis
  • 3% NaCl (hypertonic) = symptomatic hyponatremia/head trauma; give SLOWLY; monitor for pulmonary edema
  • 0.45% NaCl (hypotonic) = hypernatremia and maintenance; NOT for volume replacement (depletes ECF)
  • D5W = free water/hypernatremia; NOT a replacement fluid (provides no electrolytes, causes cell swelling)
  • Colloids pull fluid into vessels; all affect coagulation → monitor coagulation times
7 — Magnesium Toxicity Monitoring (IV Mg2+) HIGH YIELD
  • Always use infusion pump; monitor VS, LOC, and deep tendon reflexes (DTRs) frequently
  • Loss of DTRs = earliest sign of magnesium toxicity → STOP infusion
  • Keep IV calcium gluconate at bedside as antidote for severe hypermagnesemia
  • Hypomagnesemia resembles hypocalcemia: hyperactive DTRs, positive Chvostek/Trousseau, muscle cramps, torsades de pointes
  • Low Mg2+ causes refractory hypokalemia — treat Mg2+ first
8 — CVAD Priority Safety Points HIGH YIELD
  • Confirm CVAD tip position with chest x-ray before use
  • Chlorhexidine = preferred cleansing agent (longer lasting than povidone-iodine)
  • Air embolism: clamp catheter → left lateral decubitus + Trendelenburg → O2 → notify provider
  • Resistance when flushing = do NOT force (risk of rupture or dislodging thrombus)
  • Valsalva maneuver during cap changes or removal prevents air embolism
  • PICC: no BP readings or blood draws from PICC arm (risk of vein damage/thrombosis)
  • Catheter occlusion: have patient change position, raise arm, cough first; then flush with 10-mL syringe; then anticoagulant/thrombolytic
15 — Review Questions
  1. A patient has had prolonged NG suction and develops metabolic alkalosis. What is the expected ABG picture?
    Show Answer
    pH >7.45, HCO3- >26 mEq/L, PaCO2 normal or ↑ (if lungs compensating by retaining CO2). NG suction removes HCl (hydrochloric acid) → acid deficit → metabolic alkalosis. The lungs compensate by hypoventilating to retain CO2, but this is limited by hypoxemia.
  2. A 74-year-old patient with COPD has the following ABGs: pH 7.31, PaCO2 58 mm Hg, HCO3- 30 mEq/L. How do you interpret these values?
    Show Answer
    Respiratory acidosis with partial metabolic compensation. Analysis: pH 7.31 = acidosis. Using ROME: pH↓ and PaCO2↑ = OPPOSITE = Respiratory origin → Respiratory acidosis. HCO3- is 30 (elevated) → kidneys retaining HCO3- to compensate. pH still abnormal → partial (not full) compensation. In COPD, CO2 retention from air trapping/hypoventilation.
  3. A patient in the ICU has a K+ of 6.8 mEq/L. The provider orders IV calcium gluconate. The patient asks why they are getting "calcium" when the potassium is too high. How do you explain this?
    Show Answer
    Calcium gluconate does NOT lower the potassium level. It acts as a membrane stabilizer — it protects the heart from the dangerous effects of high potassium on cardiac conduction (like ventricular fibrillation). Other treatments (insulin/dextrose, albuterol, Kayexalate, dialysis) will actually reduce the potassium level.
  4. A patient returns from total thyroidectomy. Four hours post-op, she complains of tingling around her mouth and in her fingertips. What is the priority nursing assessment and action?
    Show Answer
    Assess for hypocalcemia — circumoral tingling is an early sign. Check for Chvostek sign (facial muscle twitch with facial nerve tap) and Trousseau sign (carpal spasm with BP cuff inflation). Notify the provider immediately. The parathyroid glands may have been removed or traumatized during thyroidectomy → PTH deficit → hypocalcemia. Treatment: IV calcium gluconate for symptomatic hypocalcemia. Keep calcium gluconate at the bedside for thyroidectomy patients.
  5. Why must potassium always be corrected before or alongside magnesium in a patient with both hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia?
    Show Answer
    This is backwards — it is Mg2+ that must be corrected FIRST (or alongside). Low Mg2+ stimulates renin and aldosterone release → promotes renal K+ excretion → persistent hypokalemia despite K+ replacement. If Mg2+ is not corrected, the K+ level will not stay corrected ("refractory hypokalemia").
  6. A patient develops an air embolism through a central line. Describe the appropriate immediate nursing actions and positioning.
    Show Answer
    1. Clamp the catheter immediately. 2. Place patient in left lateral decubitus (left side-lying) position with the head DOWN (Trendelenburg). This traps air in the right atrium and right ventricle, preventing it from entering the pulmonary vasculature. 3. Apply high-flow oxygen. 4. Notify the provider immediately. Monitor VS continuously.
  7. What is the difference between partial and full compensation in acid-base balance?
    Show Answer
    Partial compensation: the compensating system (lung or kidney) has kicked in and the compensatory value (PaCO2 or HCO3-) is moving in the correct direction, BUT the pH is still outside normal range (still acidotic or alkalotic). Full compensation: the compensating system has brought the pH back within normal range (7.35–7.45), though the PaCO2 and HCO3- remain abnormal. Full compensation ≠ correction; the underlying disorder still exists.
  8. A patient with renal failure and hypermagnesemia is receiving IV calcium gluconate. During the infusion the nurse notices a sudden drop in BP. What happened and what should the nurse do?
    Show Answer
    Rapid IV calcium administration can cause hypotension. The nurse should slow the infusion rate and notify the provider. Monitor BP closely throughout calcium infusion. The provider may order a slower infusion rate or an alternative formulation. Continue to monitor LOC and cardiac rhythm.
  9. Compare the ECG changes seen in hyperkalemia versus hypokalemia.
    Show Answer
    Hyperkalemia (in order): Tall peaked T waves → Loss of P waves → Prolonged PR interval → ST segment depression → Widened QRS complex → Ventricular fibrillation/standstill. Hypokalemia: Flattened T waves → ST depression → Prominent U wave (hallmark of hypokalemia) → Peaked P waves → Prolonged QRS → First/second degree heart block → Ventricular dysrhythmias. U wave is the distinguishing feature of hypokalemia.
  10. A patient is receiving isotonic saline for a fluid volume deficit. Explain why 0.9% NaCl is preferred over 0.45% NaCl for this situation.
    Show Answer
    0.9% NaCl (isotonic/normal saline) expands only the ECF compartment — it does not move into cells. This makes it ideal for restoring ECF volume in dehydrated patients. 0.45% NaCl (hypotonic) is a lower osmolality solution than plasma. When infused, it dilutes ECF, causing water to move into cells by osmosis (cells swell). This depletes the ECF compartment and can lower BP further — the opposite of what is needed in FVD. Hypotonic solutions are used for hypernatremia or maintenance, not volume replacement.
