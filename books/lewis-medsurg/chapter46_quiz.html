Question 1 of 25
Which structure is stimulated by blood-borne toxins, chemotherapy drugs, and metabolic abnormalities (such as uremia) to initiate the vomiting reflex?
Correct Answer: C — Chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) The CTZ, located in the area postrema of the medulla, lies outside the blood-brain barrier and is directly stimulated by blood-borne substances (drugs, toxins, metabolic waste). The cerebral cortex responds to pain, fear, and sights/smells. The vestibular apparatus triggers motion sickness. Vagal afferents in the gut respond to local distension and mucosal irritation.
Question 2 of 25
A patient has been vomiting persistently for 3 days. Which acid-base imbalance and electrolyte disturbance should the nurse anticipate?
Correct Answer: B — Metabolic alkalosis and hypokalemia Vomiting causes loss of hydrochloric acid (HCl) from the stomach, resulting in a deficit of hydrogen ions → metabolic alkalosis (pH >7.45, HCO3- elevated). Potassium is also lost in vomitus and through renal compensation (kidneys excrete K+ to retain H+ in alkalotic states) → hypokalemia.
Question 3 of 25
The nurse is providing health teaching to a 45-year-old patient. Which behavior places this patient at the GREATEST risk for developing oral cancer?
Correct Answer: C — Using smokeless tobacco Tobacco and alcohol use account for 75–90% of oral cancers. Smokeless tobacco (snuff, chewing tobacco) directly and continuously contacts the oral mucosa, making it a major risk factor. HPV-16 infection is also a significant emerging risk factor. Ill-fitting dentures cause chronic irritation, which is a minor contributing factor but not the greatest risk. Coffee and spicy foods are not established oral cancer risk factors.
Question 4 of 25
A patient with GERD asks the nurse what causes the burning sensation in the chest. What is the MOST accurate explanation?
Correct Answer: B — Incompetent LES allows reflux GERD results primarily from an incompetent lower esophageal sphincter (LES), which allows gastric acid and contents to reflux into the esophagus, causing mucosal irritation and the burning sensation known as heartburn. H. pylori is the primary cause of peptic ulcers, not GERD. Gastric inflammation describes gastritis. Abnormal cells in the lower esophagus describes Barrett’s esophagus, a complication of chronic GERD.
Question 5 of 25
The nurse is teaching a patient with GERD about dietary modifications. Which food or beverage should the patient be instructed to AVOID?
Correct Answer: D — Peppermint tea Peppermint relaxes the lower esophageal sphincter (LES), worsening reflux and should be avoided in GERD. Other foods/substances that relax the LES include chocolate, alcohol, caffeine, high-fat foods, and spearmint. Lean chicken, low-fat yogurt, and oatmeal are low-fat, low-acid foods that are generally well tolerated with GERD.
Question 6 of 25
A patient is diagnosed with Barrett’s esophagus after endoscopy for long-standing GERD. The nurse explains the primary clinical significance of this diagnosis is:
Correct Answer: B — Precursor to esophageal adenocarcinoma Barrett’s esophagus is a metaplastic change in which the normal squamous epithelium of the lower esophagus is replaced by columnar (glandular) epithelium. This is caused by chronic acid exposure from GERD. The columnar epithelium is a premalignant lesion with a significantly elevated risk for esophageal adenocarcinoma. Surveillance endoscopy every 3–5 years (or more frequently with dysplasia) is required. GERD treatment must continue; it does not indicate resolution.
Question 7 of 25
A patient with a known hiatal hernia develops severe substernal chest pain, dyspnea, and dysphagia that have worsened over 48 hours. Which type of hiatal hernia is MOST likely responsible and why?
Correct Answer: B — Rolling (paraesophageal) hernia with incarceration risk In a rolling (paraesophageal) hernia, the gastric fundus herniates through the hiatus alongside the esophagus while the LES remains below the diaphragm. Because the herniated segment can become trapped (incarcerated) or its blood supply cut off (strangulated), this is considered a surgical emergency. Sliding hernias (the most common type) generally cause GERD symptoms but not obstruction. Rolling hernias may actually cause less GERD but carry a higher risk of serious mechanical complications.
Question 8 of 25
A patient is admitted with acute necrotizing ulcerative gingivitis (Vincent’s infection). Which intervention is MOST appropriate for the nurse to anticipate in this patient’s care?
Correct Answer: C — Mouth irrigations, topical antibiotics, and rest Vincent’s infection (acute necrotizing ulcerative gingivitis, or "trench mouth") is a bacterial infection of the gingival tissue. Treatment includes: (1) oral irrigations with chlorhexidine and/or saline, (2) topical antibiotics, (3) physical and mental rest, (4) avoidance of smoking and alcohol, and (5) a soft, nutritious diet. Amphotericin B treats fungal infections (oral candidiasis). Viscous lidocaine is used for herpetic stomatitis. IV tetracycline is not indicated; oral antibiotics or topical therapy suffice.
Question 9 of 25
A patient reports a 2-year history of progressively worsening difficulty swallowing. He notes equal difficulty with both liquids AND solid foods from the very beginning. Which esophageal condition is MOST consistent with these findings?
Correct Answer: C — Achalasia The hallmark distinguishing feature of achalasia is dysphagia for BOTH liquids and solids from the onset, caused by failure of the LES to relax and absent peristalsis in the esophageal body. Esophageal cancer and GERD strictures cause dysphagia that begins with solid foods and progresses to liquids as obstruction worsens. Zenker’s diverticulum causes regurgitation of undigested food (often hours after eating) and gurgling sounds in the neck, not primarily equal solid/liquid dysphagia.
Question 10 of 25
A patient newly diagnosed with esophageal cancer asks the nurse what symptoms typically appear first. Which response is MOST accurate?
Correct Answer: B — Progressive dysphagia, solids first then liquids The classic presenting symptom of esophageal cancer is progressive dysphagia — initially for solid foods, then for soft foods, and eventually for liquids. By the time dysphagia is noticeable, the tumor typically occupies ≥60% of the esophageal lumen, which explains why most esophageal cancer is diagnosed at a late stage. Weight loss is also a hallmark. Heartburn may precede Barrett’s → adenocarcinoma but is not the presenting symptom of cancer itself.
Question 11 of 25
A patient with peptic ulcer disease reports burning epigastric pain that occurs 2 to 3 hours after meals and is relieved when he eats a snack. Where is the ulcer MOST likely located?
Correct Answer: C — Duodenal ulcer Duodenal ulcers classically cause pain 2–3 hours after meals (when the stomach has emptied and acid contacts the ulcerated duodenal wall) that is relieved by eating (food buffers the acid). Duodenal ulcers may also cause pain that wakes patients at night. Gastric ulcers produce pain 30–60 minutes after eating (food stimulates more acid release against the ulcerated gastric wall), and eating does NOT relieve the pain.
Question 12 of 25
The nurse reviews the medication record of a patient being treated for H. pylori-associated peptic ulcer disease. Which combination is CONSISTENT with standard first-line triple therapy?
Correct Answer: A — PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin Standard first-line triple therapy for H. pylori consists of a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) + clarithromycin + amoxicillin (or metronidazole if penicillin-allergic), taken for 10–14 days. Quadruple therapy (PPI + bismuth + metronidazole + tetracycline) is used when triple therapy fails. Ranitidine is an H2 blocker (less potent than PPIs); ciprofloxacin is not part of standard H. pylori regimens; sucralfate is a mucosal coating agent, not an antibiotic.
Question 13 of 25
A patient with a history of peptic ulcer disease suddenly develops severe, sharp pain that rapidly becomes diffuse throughout the abdomen. On assessment the nurse finds a rigid, board-like abdomen. What complication should the nurse suspect, and what is the PRIORITY action?
Correct Answer: C — Perforation; maintain NPO, notify provider, prepare for surgery Sudden, severe abdominal pain with a rigid, board-like abdomen is the classic presentation of a perforated ulcer (peritonitis). Gastric contents spill into the peritoneal cavity, causing chemical peritonitis that rapidly becomes bacterial peritonitis. This is a surgical emergency. Immediate actions: keep NPO, establish IV access, insert NG tube, notify surgeon, administer IV antibiotics, and prepare the patient for emergency laparotomy or laparoscopic repair.
Question 14 of 25
A patient with a gastric ulcer develops sudden tachycardia, hypotension, and vomiting of bright red blood. Which nursing action is the HIGHEST priority?
Correct Answer: B — Large-bore IV access, NPO, call provider The priority follows the ABCs: the patient is hemodynamically unstable (tachycardia, hypotension). Two large-bore IV lines allow rapid volume replacement. NPO status prevents further injury and prepares for possible endoscopy or surgery. Calling the provider ensures prompt intervention. A PPI bolus is indicated but is a secondary action. Trendelenburg is contraindicated in active GI bleeding (risk of aspiration with vomiting). NG lavage is a subsequent nursing action ordered by the provider.
Question 15 of 25
A patient with longstanding PUD develops projectile vomiting of undigested food eaten 10 hours earlier. Pyloric obstruction is suspected. Which metabolic imbalance should the nurse monitor for?
Correct Answer: B — Metabolic alkalosis and hypokalemia Prolonged vomiting from any cause (including pyloric obstruction) results in loss of hydrochloric acid (HCl) → metabolic alkalosis. Potassium is also lost in vomitus and excreted by the kidneys in exchange for H+ during compensation → hypokalemia. This is the same imbalance seen in any prolonged vomiting state. Metabolic acidosis would occur with diarrhea (bicarbonate loss).
Question 16 of 25
A patient with chronic atrophic gastritis is at risk for pernicious anemia. The nurse explains this risk is caused by:
Correct Answer: C — Loss of intrinsic factor from parietal cell destruction Chronic atrophic gastritis (particularly Type A, autoimmune) causes destruction of the gastric parietal cells. Parietal cells produce both hydrochloric acid AND intrinsic factor. Intrinsic factor is essential for binding vitamin B12 (cobalamin) in the stomach so it can be absorbed in the terminal ileum. Without intrinsic factor, B12 cannot be absorbed → megaloblastic (pernicious) anemia. Treatment is lifelong vitamin B12 injections or high-dose oral B12 (if some absorption remains).
Question 17 of 25
A patient who had a Billroth II procedure 3 weeks ago reports profuse sweating, weakness, palpitations, and severe abdominal cramping within 20 minutes of finishing lunch. Which complication should the nurse suspect, and what is the priority dietary instruction?
Correct Answer: B — Early dumping syndrome; lie down after meals, no fluids with meals Early dumping syndrome occurs within 15–30 minutes after eating due to rapid emptying of hyperosmolar gastric contents into the jejunum. The osmotic gradient draws fluid into the bowel, causing decreased circulating volume and autonomic symptoms (sweating, palpitations, weakness) and GI symptoms (cramping, diarrhea). Key teaching: eat 6 small dry meals per day, eliminate fluids with meals (drink 30–45 min after), avoid concentrated sweets, and lie down for 30 minutes after eating to slow transit. Late dumping occurs 1–3 hours after eating and is caused by reactive hypoglycemia.
Question 18 of 25
Two hours after lunch, a patient who had a total gastrectomy 6 weeks ago develops diaphoresis, trembling, and confusion. What should the nurse do FIRST?
Correct Answer: B — Check blood glucose level first The symptoms occurring 1–3 hours after a meal in a postgastrectomy patient are classic for late dumping syndrome, which causes postprandial reactive hypoglycemia (rapid glucose absorption → excess insulin release → hypoglycemia). However, the nurse must ASSESS before treating: verify the blood glucose level before administering sugar. If BG is confirmed low, then offer a carbohydrate snack (if the patient can swallow safely) or IV dextrose if the patient is confused/unable to swallow. Giving D50W without checking could cause hyperglycemia if the diagnosis is wrong.
Question 19 of 25
The nurse is assessing a patient with suspected stomach (gastric) cancer. Which statement BEST describes why this cancer is often diagnosed at an advanced stage?
Correct Answer: C — Early symptoms are vague and attributed to benign conditions Stomach cancer has an insidious onset. Early disease causes only vague symptoms such as mild indigestion, epigastric discomfort, bloating, and early satiety — symptoms easily dismissed as GERD or peptic ulcer disease. By the time more specific symptoms appear (anorexia, weight loss, palpable mass, ascites), the disease is often unresectable. This late presentation contributes to the poor 5-year survival rate for gastric cancer in Western countries.
Question 20 of 25
A patient with known cirrhosis and esophageal varices suddenly vomits 400 mL of bright red blood. What is the PRIORITY nursing action?
Correct Answer: C — Maintain airway and establish IV access The priority in any acute GI hemorrhage follows the ABCs. Massive hematemesis creates an immediate risk of airway obstruction and aspiration. The first action is airway protection (position, suction available, possible intubation). Simultaneously, large-bore IV access is needed for rapid volume replacement. Octreotide, endoscopy, and NG tube insertion are all appropriate subsequent interventions, but none supersede airway management.
Question 21 of 25
A patient with PUD is prescribed omeprazole. Which statement BEST describes the mechanism of action of this drug?
Correct Answer: D — Irreversible inhibition of H+/K+ ATPase (proton pump) Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) such as omeprazole, lansoprazole, and pantoprazole work by irreversibly binding to and inhibiting the H+/K+ ATPase enzyme (the proton pump) in the parietal cell canaliculus. This is the final common step in acid secretion regardless of the stimulus (histamine, acetylcholine, gastrin). PPIs provide more complete and sustained acid suppression than H2 blockers. Neutralization describes antacids; protective coating describes sucralfate; H2 receptor blockade describes ranitidine/famotidine.
Question 22 of 25
A patient asks the nurse when to take antacids for MAXIMUM therapeutic benefit for peptic ulcer symptoms. What is the BEST response?
Correct Answer: B — 1–3 hours after meals and at bedtime Antacids taken 1–3 hours after meals provide the longest duration of acid neutralization (up to 3–4 hours) because food buffers the antacid and prolongs its gastric emptying time. Taken at bedtime, they neutralize the nocturnal acid surge. If taken with meals, antacids have a shorter effect as they empty with food. Taking on an empty stomach produces only a brief effect (∼30 minutes) before rapid gastric emptying occurs.
Question 23 of 25
Several members of a family develop nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea approximately 2 to 3 hours after a summer picnic. There is no fever. Which food source and causative agent are MOST consistent with this presentation?
Correct Answer: B — Staphylococcal toxin from mayonnaise/potato salad The rapid onset (30 minutes–7 hours) and absence of fever point to a pre-formed toxin rather than an active infection. Staphylococcus aureus produces a heat-stable enterotoxin in protein-rich foods (mayonnaise, cream fillings, potato salad, meat) left at warm temperatures. The toxin — not the bacteria itself — causes the GI illness, hence no fever. Botulism has a longer onset and causes neurologic symptoms. E. coli O157:H7 has a longer incubation and causes bloody diarrhea. Salmonella causes fever and has a longer onset.
Question 24 of 25
A patient taking daily ibuprofen for chronic arthritis is prescribed misoprostol. The nurse explains the purpose of misoprostol is to:
Correct Answer: B — Cytoprotection via prostaglandin replacement NSAIDs (like ibuprofen) inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, reducing prostaglandin synthesis. In the gastric mucosa, prostaglandins normally stimulate mucus and bicarbonate secretion (the mucosal protective barrier) and maintain mucosal blood flow. Loss of these prostaglandins leads to mucosal erosion and ulceration. Misoprostol is a synthetic prostaglandin E1 analog that replaces this protective prostaglandin activity, reducing NSAID-induced mucosal damage. PPIs are an alternative for gastroprotection with NSAIDs. Misoprostol does NOT enhance ibuprofen’s anti-inflammatory effect and is contraindicated in pregnancy (causes uterine contractions).
Question 25 of 25
A patient with a Zenker’s (pharyngeal) diverticulum asks the nurse why food seems to “come back up” hours after eating and why there is a gurgling sensation in the throat. What is the MOST accurate explanation?
Correct Answer: B — Esophageal diverticulum trapping food A Zenker’s (pharyngeal) diverticulum is an outpouching that forms at the junction of the pharynx and esophagus. Food accumulates in the pouch during eating and is then regurgitated hours later (halitosis from fermenting food is common). The gurgling or “bubbling” sensation (borborygmi) in the neck is caused by air and food moving in and out of the pouch. Serious complications include aspiration, malnutrition, and perforation. Treatment is surgical repair of the diverticulum.
