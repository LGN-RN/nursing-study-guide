1. Overview, Concepts & Learning Outcomes

Learning Outcomes

  1. Describe the etiology, complications, and nursing management of nausea and vomiting.
  2. Discuss oral inflammations/infections and nursing management of oral cancer.
  3. Explain types, pathophysiology, complications, and management of GERD and hiatal hernia.
  4. Relate pathophysiology, manifestations, and management of esophageal cancer, diverticula, achalasia, and strictures.
  5. Compare acute and chronic gastritis including etiology and management.
  6. Distinguish gastric and duodenal ulcers including manifestations, complications, and management.
  7. Outline clinical manifestations and management of stomach cancer.
  8. Explain common etiologies, manifestations, and management of upper GI bleeding.
  9. Identify common foodborne illnesses and nursing responsibilities.

Conceptual Focus

  • Fluid and Electrolytes — vomiting, GI bleeding, and surgical complications all disrupt fluid/electrolyte balance
  • Nutrition — malnutrition risk in oral cancer, esophageal cancer, stomach cancer, and postgastrectomy patients
  • Pain — epigastric pain is the hallmark of PUD; pain patterns differ between gastric and duodenal ulcers
  • Sleep — nocturnal GERD and reflux-related cough disrupt sleep
  • Tissue Integrity — mucosal breakdown underlies gastritis, PUD, and GERD

2. Nausea and Vomiting

Pathophysiology

  • The vomiting center (medulla) coordinates the vomiting reflex via autonomic afferent pathways from GI tract, kidneys, heart, and uterus
  • The chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) in the brainstem responds to chemical stimuli (drugs, toxins, labyrinthine stimulation) and transmits to the vomiting center
  • Sympathetic activation → tachycardia, tachypnea, diaphoresis
  • Parasympathetic activation → LES relaxation, ↑ gastric motility, ↑ saliva

Common Causes

  • GI disorders, pregnancy, CNS problems, CVD (MI, HF)
  • Psychologic states (stress, fear), overdistended GI tract
  • Postoperative (general anesthesia), chemotherapy, opioids
  • Women more susceptible to anesthesia-related and motion sickness N/V

Clinical Manifestations & Complications

  • Anorexia usually accompanies nausea
  • Prolonged vomiting → dehydration, electrolyte loss (K+, Na+, Cl-)
  • Metabolic alkalosis — from loss of gastric HCl acid NCLEX
  • Metabolic acidosis — less common; from loss of small intestine contents
  • Severe: circulatory failure, weight loss
Drug Alerts Promethazine: Avoid parenteral routes (risk of severe tissue injury). Use oral or rectal only.
Metoclopramide (Reglan): Chronic use or high doses → risk for tardive dyskinesia (involuntary repetitive movements; may persist after stopping).

Nursing Management

Assessment Priorities

  • Character/color of vomitus: bright red = active bleeding; coffee-grounds = gastric bleeding; fecal odor = intestinal obstruction; bile = obstruction below ampulla of Vater
  • Distinguish vomiting vs. regurgitation vs. projectile vomiting
  • Timing: early morning → pregnancy; cyclic vomiting syndrome → recurring episodes with fatigue

Acute Care

  • NPO, IV fluids with electrolyte/glucose replacement until tolerating oral intake
  • NG tube to suction if persistent vomiting, bowel obstruction, or paralytic ileus
  • Semi-Fowler's or side-lying position to prevent aspiration (especially older adults)
  • Monitor I&O; assess for dehydration signs; provide oral care

Nutrition Reintroduction

  • Start with small sips of water (5–15 mL every 15–20 min) at room temperature
  • Progress to dry toast, crackers, plain gelatin; high-carb, low-fat diet
  • Avoid very hot/cold, coffee, spicy foods, strong odors
  • Liquids between (not with) meals to reduce distention
Gerontologic Consideration Older adults are at higher risk for life-threatening fluid/electrolyte imbalances and aspiration. Antiemetics cause confusion and increase fall risk in older adults — reduce doses and use safety precautions.

Oral Cancer

Epidemiology

  • ~51,540 new cases/year in the US; ~10,030 deaths
  • More common after age 35; average diagnosis age 65; 2× more common in men
  • 5-year survival: 84% localized; 65% all stages
  • Health equity: highest incidence and mortality in Black men
  • Most lesions on lower lip; also tongue, buccal mucosa

Risk Factors (Table 46.4)

  • Tobacco use (75–90%): smoking, snuff, chewing tobacco
  • Chronic alcohol use
  • HPV (human papillomavirus) — 25% of cases; multiple oral sex partners
  • Prolonged sun exposure (lip cancer)
  • Poor oral hygiene, chronic irritation

Clinical Manifestations

  • Leukoplakia — white patch ("smoker's patch"); 15% become cancerous
  • Erythroplasia (erythroplakia) — red velvety patch; >50% progress to squamous cell cancer HIGH RISK
  • Indurated, painless ulcer (lip); ulcer/thickening of tongue
  • Late signs: increased saliva, slurred speech, dysphagia, toothache, earache
  • Asymptomatic neck mass in ~30%

Diagnostics

  • Oral exfoliative cytology (scrape and slide)
  • Toluidine blue test — cancer cells take up dye
  • CT, MRI, PET for staging after confirmed diagnosis

Treatment

  • Surgery (often radical): partial mandibulectomy, hemiglossectomy, glossectomy, radical neck dissection
  • Radiation therapy (~6 weeks post-surgery)
  • Chemotherapy: fluorouracil, cisplatin, carboplatin, paclitaxel
  • Nutrition: PEG/EN before radiation; PN for 24–48 hr post-radical surgery, then EN via NG/gastrostomy
Any ulcerative oral lesion that does not heal within 2–3 weeks should be referred to the HCP for evaluation. Leukoplakia does NOT always hurt — teach patients to report it promptly.
4. Esophageal Problems (GERD, Hiatal Hernia, Esophageal Cancer, Achalasia & More)

GERD — Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease

GERD is the most common upper GI problem. It is a syndrome (not a disease) caused by reflux of gastric acid into the lower esophagus that overwhelms esophageal defenses. About 15 million Americans have symptoms daily.

Pathophysiology

  • Primary cause: incompetent lower esophageal sphincter (LES) — allows gastric contents to reflux when supine or with ↑ intraabdominal pressure
  • Gastric HCl acid and pepsin cause esophageal irritation/inflammation (esophagitis)
  • Factors that decrease LES pressure: alcohol, chocolate, fatty foods, caffeine, nicotine, peppermint/spearmint, calcium channel blockers, nitrates, progesterone, anticholinergics, β-blockers, morphine
  • Factors that increase LES pressure: bethanechol (Urecholine), metoclopramide (Reglan)
  • Obesity ↑ intraabdominal pressure → worsens GERD

Clinical Manifestations

  • Heartburn (pyrosis) — most common; burning beneath lower sternum after eating/lying down
  • Dyspepsia (upper abdominal discomfort), regurgitation (hot/bitter/sour liquid in throat)
  • Respiratory symptoms: wheezing, cough, dyspnea; laryngospasm, hoarseness
  • GERD-related chest pain mimics angina but relieved by antacids (unlike angina)
  • Evaluate heartburn >2×/week or severe/nocturnal

Complications

  • Esophagitis → scar tissue formation, stricture, dysphagia
  • Barrett Esophagus (BE) — squamous → columnar cell metaplasia; risk for esophageal adenocarcinoma NCLEX
    • 5–30% of chronic GERD develop BE
    • Risk factors: age >60, male, white, central obesity
    • Requires surveillance endoscopy or radiofrequency ablation
  • Respiratory complications: asthma, chronic bronchitis, aspiration pneumonia
  • Dental erosion (posterior teeth) from acid reflux

Diagnostics (Table 46.8)

  • Often diagnosed based on symptoms and response to therapy
  • Upper GI endoscopy — assess LES competence, inflammation, strictures; biopsy for cancer/BE
  • Manometry — measures LES pressure and esophageal motility
  • Ambulatory esophageal pH monitoring — for refractory symptoms without mucosal inflammation
  • Esophagram (barium swallow) — hiatal hernia

Management (Table 46.8)

GERD Lifestyle Teaching (Table 46.9)
  • Low-fat diet; eat small, frequent meals
  • Avoid alcohol, caffeine
  • Stop smoking (almost immediate improvement in LES pressure)
  • Do NOT lie down for 2–3 hours after eating
  • Avoid eating within 3 hours of bedtime
  • Sleep with HOB elevated 30 degrees (4–6 inch blocks or pillows, not just pillow wedge)
  • Avoid tight clothing around waist, bending over after eating
  • Reduce weight if overweight

Drug Therapy (see Pharmacology section): PPIs are most effective; H2 receptor blockers; antacids for mild/intermittent symptoms; prokinetics for delayed gastric emptying

Surgical Therapy — for complications unresponsive to medical management: Nissen fundoplication (fundus wrapped around lower esophagus) or Toupet fundoplication — usually laparoscopic. LINX system (ring of magnets around LES) for refractory symptoms; patients cannot have MRI.

Hiatal Hernia

Herniation of part of the stomach through the diaphragmatic hiatus. Most common abnormality on upper GI x-ray. More common in older adults and women.

Types

  • Sliding (most common) — gastroesophageal junction slides above the diaphragm; returns to normal position when upright
  • Paraesophageal (rolling) — fundus/greater curvature rolls up through diaphragm alongside esophagus; GEJ stays in normal position. EMERGENCY if acute (strangulation)

Management

  • Conservative therapy same as GERD
  • Reduce intraabdominal pressure — avoid constricting garments, lifting, straining
  • Surgical repair (laparoscopic Nissen/Toupet): herniotomy, herniorrhaphy, fundoplication, gastropexy

Esophageal Cancer

Epidemiology

  • 18,400 new cases and 16,170 deaths in 2020; 5-year survival ~20%
  • Mostly adenocarcinoma (lower esophagus); some squamous cell (upper)
  • Most common between ages 65–75; more common in men
  • Health equity: highest in non-Hispanic white men

Risk Factors & Pathophysiology

  • Barrett esophagus, smoking, excess alcohol, obesity
  • Achalasia associated with squamous cell cancer
  • Most tumors in middle and lower esophagus; spreads via lymph system; metastasis to liver and lungs
  • Often diagnosed at advanced stage

Clinical Manifestations

  • Progressive dysphagia — most common symptom; starts with meats → soft foods → liquids HALLMARK
  • Substernal/epigastric/back pain (late); radiates to neck, jaw, ears, shoulders
  • Weight loss, regurgitation of blood-flecked contents
  • Odynophagia (burning/squeezing pain while swallowing)
  • Complications: bleeding (aortic erosion), perforation with fistula, obstruction

Management

  • Multimodal: surgery + chemotherapy + radiation
  • Surgery: esophagectomy, esophagogastrostomy, esophagoenterostomy
  • Minimally invasive esophagectomy: fewer pulmonary complications, shorter ICU stay
  • Endoscopic: photodynamic therapy (porfimer sodium [Photofrin] + light), EMR, radiofrequency ablation, dilation/stent placement
  • Chemo drugs: carboplatin/paclitaxel, cisplatin/irinotecan, DCF (docetaxel/cisplatin/fluorouracil)
  • Targeted therapy: trastuzumab (HER-2+), ramucirumab (VEGF inhibitor, GEJ cancers)
  • Post-esophagectomy: swallowing study before starting oral fluids; upright position 2 hr after eating; watch for anastomotic leak (fever, pain, dyspnea)
Postoperative Alert — Anastomotic Leak Signs: tachycardia, dyspnea, fever, abdominal pain, anxiety, restlessness. Requires immediate treatment to prevent sepsis and death.

Other Esophageal Disorders

Eosinophilic Esophagitis (EoE)

  • Eosinophil infiltration of esophagus; personal/family history of allergies
  • Food triggers: milk, egg, wheat, rye, beef
  • Symptoms: heartburn, dysphagia, food impaction, N/V, weight loss
  • Treatment: avoid trigger foods; PPIs; corticosteroids (fluticasone — swallowed, not inhaled; topical delivery to esophagus)
  • Side effect of swallowed fluticasone: esophageal candidiasis

Esophageal Diverticula (Outpouchings)

  • 3 types: Zenker's (above upper esophageal sphincter — most common; age >60), traction (near midpoint), epiphrenic (above LES)
  • Symptoms: dysphagia, chronic cough, regurgitation, aspiration, weight loss, foul odor
  • Diagnosis: barium studies, endoscopy
  • Surgery if nutrition disrupted: endoscopic stapling diverticulotomy

Esophageal Strictures

  • Most common cause: chronic GERD; also caustic ingestion, radiation, surgical anastomosis, trauma
  • Causes dysphagia and regurgitation → weight loss
  • Treatment: dilation (mechanical bougies or balloons), surgical resection

Achalasia

  • Absent peristalsis in lower 2/3 of esophagus; ↑ LES pressure with incomplete relaxation
  • Selective loss of inhibitory neurons → unopposed LES contraction
  • Dysphagia for BOTH liquids and solids DISTINGUISHING FEATURE
  • Also: globus sensation, substernal chest pain, nocturnal regurgitation, halitosis, weight loss
  • Diagnosis: esophagram (barium swallow), high-resolution manometry, endoscopy
  • Treatment:
    • Endoscopic pneumatic dilation (3.0–4.0 cm balloons)
    • Heller myotomy (laparoscopic LES muscle incision) — often with anti-reflux surgery
    • Botulinum toxin injection — short-term; for older patients with comorbidities
    • Medications (less effective): nitrates, calcium channel blockers (sublingually 10–30 min before meals)
5. Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)

PUD = erosion of GI mucosa from digestive action of HCl acid and pepsin. Affects ~4.6 million Americans/year. Occurs in lower esophagus, stomach, duodenum, or gastrojejunal anastomosis.

Types

  • Acute ulcer — superficial erosion, minimal inflammation; short duration; resolves when cause removed
  • Chronic ulcer — long duration; erodes through muscular wall with fibrous tissue; more common than acute
  • Classified by location: gastric or duodenal

Etiology & Pathophysiology

H. pylori

  • Major risk factor: 80% gastric / 90% duodenal ulcers related to H. pylori NCLEX
  • Bacteria colonize gastric epithelial cells; produce urease → ammonium chloride + inflammatory cytokines → ↑ gastric secretion → tissue damage
  • Most infections acquired in childhood; transmission likely fecal-oral or oral-oral

NSAIDs

  • Most common cause of non-H. pylori PUD
  • Inhibit prostaglandin synthesis → ↑ gastric acid secretion, ↓ mucosal barrier integrity
  • Risk ↑ with concurrent corticosteroids or anticoagulants

Lifestyle Factors

  • Alcohol → acute mucosal lesions; smoking → delays ulcer healing
  • Coffee → strong stimulant of gastric acid secretion

Duodenal Ulcer Risk Conditions

  • COPD, cirrhosis, pancreatitis, hyperparathyroidism, chronic kidney disease
  • Zollinger-Ellison Syndrome (ZES) — gastrinoma causing severe peptic ulceration and HCl acid hypersecretion

Diagnostics

  • Endoscopy — most accurate; direct view; biopsy for H. pylori and to rule out stomach cancer
  • H. pylori testing: urea breath test (identifies active infection — best noninvasive), stool antigen test, serology (stays positive years — not for evaluating treatment), rapid urease testing of biopsy (gold standard)
  • CBC — anemia from bleeding; liver enzymes; serum amylase (posterior duodenal ulcer → pancreas)
  • Barium contrast for obstruction or patients who cannot undergo endoscopy
  • Fasting serum gastrin — for suspected gastrinoma (ZES)

Complications

1. GI Bleeding (most common)

Duodenal ulcers cause more bleeding episodes than gastric ulcers.

2. Perforation (most lethal) NCLEX

  • Highest risk with large penetrating duodenal ulcers
  • Sudden, severe upper abdominal pain spreading throughout abdomen; radiates to back/shoulders; rigid, board-like abdomen; absent bowel sounds EMERGENCY
  • Without treatment: bacterial peritonitis within 6–12 hours
  • Management: NG tube decompression; IV fluids/albumin; packed RBCs; broad-spectrum antibiotics; surgical closure if doesn't seal spontaneously

3. Gastric Outlet Obstruction

  • Result of edema, inflammation, pylorospasm, or scar tissue formation
  • Pain worse at end of day; projectile vomiting with food from hours/days before
  • Management: NG tube decompression until edema resolves; IV fluid/electrolyte replacement; balloon dilation; surgery for scar tissue
  • Residual <200 mL before resuming oral intake

Conservative Management

  • Adequate rest, smoking cessation, alcohol reduction, diet modifications
  • Stop aspirin/NSAIDs for 4–6 weeks; if aspirin must continue → PPI, H2 blocker, or misoprostol co-administered
  • Pain disappears in 3–6 days; complete healing 3–9 weeks
  • Follow-up endoscopy at 3–6 months after diagnosis

6. Gastritis

Gastritis = inflammation of the gastric mucosa. May be acute or chronic, diffuse or localized. Caused by breakdown of the normal gastric mucosal barrier → back diffusion of HCl acid → tissue edema, capillary disruption, bleeding.

Risk Factors & Causes (Table 46.19)

CategoryExamples
DrugsNSAIDs, aspirin, corticosteroids, digitalis, iron supplements, bisphosphonates
DietAlcohol (most common), large amounts of spicy/irritating foods
MicroorganismsH. pylori, CMV, Salmonella, Staphylococcus, Mycobacterium, Treponema pallidum (syphilis)
EnvironmentalRadiation, smoking
DiseasesBurns, Crohn disease, large hiatal hernia, physiologic stress, sepsis, shock, renal failure
OtherBile reflux (post-surgery), endoscopy, NG tube, emotional stress/CNS lesions

Autoimmune Gastritis

  • Immune response against parietal cells → loss of parietal cells → ↓ intrinsic factor
  • → Cobalamin (vitamin B12) malabsorption → pernicious anemia NCLEX
  • ↑ Risk for stomach cancer
  • More common in women of northern European descent

Clinical Manifestations

  • Acute: anorexia, nausea/vomiting, epigastric tenderness, feeling of fullness; GI bleeding (especially with alcohol); self-limiting hours to days
  • Chronic: similar to acute; may be asymptomatic; parietal cell atrophy → loss of intrinsic factor → pernicious anemia

Management

Acute Gastritis

  • Eliminate cause (NPO, IV fluids, antiemetics)
  • In severe cases: NG tube to monitor/lavage/keep stomach empty
  • Drug therapy: H2 receptor blockers (cimetidine) or PPIs (omeprazole) to ↓ gastric acid secretion

Chronic Gastritis

  • Identify and eliminate specific cause; alcohol cessation, stop offending drugs
  • H. pylori: antibiotic combinations (Table 46.14)
  • Pernicious anemia: lifelong cobalamin replacement therapy
  • Small, frequent (6/day) nonirritating meals; smoking contraindicated in all forms of gastritis
7. Stomach Cancer & GI Stromal Tumors

Stomach (Gastric) Cancer

Epidemiology

  • ~27,600 new cases and ~11,010 deaths/year; average diagnosis age 68
  • 2:1 male:female ratio; higher in Asian/Pacific Islanders, Blacks, Hispanics
  • Only 10–20% confined to stomach at diagnosis (5-year survival 71%); >50% have advanced metastatic disease (overall 5-year survival ~32%)
  • Stomach cancer spreads by direct extension → liver, then peritoneal seeding

Risk Factors

  • H. pylori infection (→ dysplasia cascade)
  • Smoking, obesity, smoked/salted/pickled foods
  • Atrophic gastritis, pernicious anemia, adenomatous polyps, achlorhydria
  • MALT lymphoma, first-degree family history
  • Hereditary diffuse gastric cancer (HDGC) — 1% of stomach cancers
  • Whole grains and fresh fruits/vegetables are protective

Clinical Manifestations

  • Often spreads to adjacent organs before symptoms occur
  • Unexplained weight loss, indigestion, abdominal discomfort/pain
  • Anemia (chronic blood loss or pernicious anemia from intrinsic factor loss)
  • Early satiety (feeling full sooner than usual)
  • Hard, enlarged supraclavicular lymph nodes → metastasis via thoracic duct
  • Ascites → poor prognostic sign

Diagnostics (Table 46.17)

  • Upper GI endoscopy with biopsy — best diagnostic tool
  • EUS, CT, MRI, PET — staging; laparoscopy for peritoneal spread
  • Blood: anemia (CBC), ↑ liver enzymes (liver involvement), ↑ amylase (pancreas)
  • Tumor markers: α-fetoprotein, CA-19-9, CA-125, CA 72-4, CEA

Management

  • Surgery (treatment of choice): partial or total gastrectomy
    • Antrum/pyloric lesions: Billroth I (gastroduodenostomy) or Billroth II (gastrojejunostomy)
    • Fundus lesions: total gastrectomy with esophagojejunostomy
  • Chemotherapy: fluorouracil, capecitabine, carboplatin, cisplatin, docetaxel, irinotecan; ECF combination
  • Targeted therapy: trastuzumab (HER-2+), ramucirumab (VEGF)
  • Preoperative: correct anemia (packed RBCs), nutritional support (EN/PN)

Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumors (GISTs)

  • Rare cancer originating in interstitial cells of Cajal in GI tract wall
  • ~60% in stomach, ~30% small intestine; occur ages 50–70
  • Early symptoms subtle: early satiety, fatigue, bloating, N/V, change in bowel habits
  • Later: GI bleeding, obstruction from larger tumors
  • Surgery = only permanent cure; unresponsive to conventional chemotherapy
  • Tyrosine kinase inhibitors effective: imatinib (Gleevec), sunitinib, regorafenib

Postoperative Nursing Care

  • NG tube for decompression — observe aspirate color (bloody → yellow-green within 36–48 hr)
  • Report: bright red bleeding not decreasing after 2–3 hr; bleeding >75 mL/hr
  • Never irrigate, reposition, or reinsert NG tube without HCP order (risk of perforating anastomosis)
  • Watch for anastomotic leak (tachycardia, dyspnea, fever, pain) — immediately notify HCP
  • Respiratory: deep breathing, cough, incentive spirometry q2h; splint with pillow
  • Daily weights; measure I&O; early ambulation

Long-Term Postoperative Complications

1. Dumping Syndrome NCLEX

  • Direct result of removal of large stomach portion and pyloric sphincter
  • Large bolus of hypertonic fluid enters intestine → fluid drawn into bowel lumen → ↓ plasma volume, bowel distension, rapid intestinal transit
  • Symptoms within 15–30 minutes after eating: weakness, sweating, palpitations, dizziness, abdominal cramps, borborygmi, urge to defecate EARLY DUMPING
  • Diet management: 6 small feedings; no fluids with meals (30–45 min before/after); avoid concentrated sweets; lie down 30 min after eating; avoid carbonated beverages

2. Postprandial Hypoglycemia (Late Dumping Syndrome)

  • Rapid gastric emptying of high-carb bolus → hyperglycemia → excess insulin release → rebound hypoglycemia
  • Symptoms ~2 hours after eating: sweating, weakness, mental confusion, palpitations, tachycardia, anxiety LATE DUMPING
  • Treatment: limit sugars per meal; small, frequent meals; immediate sugared fluids/candy for hypoglycemic episode

3. Bile Reflux Gastritis

  • Removal/reconstruction of pylorus → bile reflux → chronic gastritis
  • Continuous epigastric distress increasing after meals; vomiting provides temporary relief
  • Treatment: cholestyramine (before/with meals) to bind bile salts

4. Pernicious Anemia (Long-term)

  • Loss of parietal cells (intrinsic factor source) after total gastrectomy (or partial)
  • Intrinsic factor → cobalamin absorption in terminal ileum → RBC growth/maturation
  • Requires lifelong cobalamin replacement + multivitamins (folate, calcium, vitamin D, iron)
Postgastrectomy Nutrition Principles (Table 46.18)
  • 6 small feedings/day; low roughage; high complex carbs and protein
  • No fluids with meals (wait 30–45 min before/after)
  • Avoid concentrated sweets, carbonated beverages, fried foods, lactose
  • Chew food thoroughly; avoid extreme temperatures
9. Upper GI Bleeding

Incidence: 103 cases per 100,000 adults/year. Mortality: 14% in hospitalized patients; 11% in ED. Mortality increases with age.

Types of Upper GI Bleeding (Table 46.20)

TypeManifestationClinical Significance
Obvious — HematemesisBright red = active bleeding; coffee-grounds = blood in stomach for some timeIndicates upper GI source above ligament of Treitz
Obvious — MelenaBlack, tarry stools (foul-smelling); iron from hemoglobin breakdownSlow bleeding from upper GI; longer transit = darker stool
OccultSmall amounts not visible; detected by guaiac testChronic slow blood loss

Common Causes (Table 46.21)

  • Stomach/Duodenum: PUD (H. pylori + NSAIDs — most common), drug-induced (NSAIDs, corticosteroids, salicylates), erosive gastritis, SRMD, stomach cancer, polyps
  • Esophagus: esophageal varices (from cirrhosis), esophagitis, Mallory-Weiss tear
  • Systemic: blood dyscrasias (leukemia, aplastic anemia), renal failure
Stress-Related Mucosal Disease (SRMD) Mucosal damage in critically ill patients who have severe burns, trauma, or major surgery. Patients at highest risk: coagulopathy, liver disease, organ failure, renal replacement therapy.

Diagnostics

  • Endoscopy — primary tool for diagnosis; within first 24 hours of bleeding
  • Angiography — when endoscopy cannot be done or bleeding persists
  • Labs: CBC, BUN, electrolytes, PT/PTT, liver enzymes, ABGs, type & crossmatch
  • Initial hematocrit may be normal — does not reflect loss until 4–6 hr after fluid replacement
  • ↑ BUN — GI bacteria break down protein; also may indicate renal hypoperfusion

Emergency Management (Table 46.23)

  • Massive hemorrhage = loss of >1500 mL or 25% of intravascular volume; 80–85% stop spontaneously
  • 2 large-bore IV lines (16–18 gauge); isotonic crystalloid (lactated Ringer's) initially
  • O2 via nasal cannula or nonrebreather mask; ECG monitoring
  • Insert NG tube; insert indwelling urinary catheter (monitor hourly output)
  • IV PPI therapy to ↓ acid secretion (before endoscopy)
  • Blood replacement: packed RBCs, fresh frozen plasma for massive hemorrhage
  • Vital signs q15–30 min; signs of shock: ↓ BP, rapid/weak pulse, cold/clammy skin, restlessness

Endoscopic Hemostasis (First-Line)

  • Mechanical: clips or bands — directly compress bleeding vessel
  • Thermal ablation: cauterize tissue using heat (YAG laser, electrocoagulation, heater probes, argon plasma coagulation)
  • Injection: epinephrine injection
  • Variceal bleeding: ligation, injection sclerotherapy, balloon tamponade

Surgical Therapy

  • Indicated when bleeding continues despite therapy and site is identified
  • May be done if patient needs >2000 mL blood or still in shock after 24 hours

Nursing Management

Assessment Priorities
  • Assess level of consciousness, vital signs, skin color, capillary refill
  • Abdomen for distention, guarding, peristalsis (absent bowel sounds → perforation)
  • Color of emesis and stools: black/tarry = prolonged bleeding; bright red stool without occult emesis = short duration
  • Watch for delirium tremens in alcohol-related bleeds (agitation, shaking, sweating, hallucinations)

Gastric Lavage

  • Effectiveness controversial; when used: 50–100 mL instilled at a time
  • Stop if resistance felt (NG tip against mucosa) — use gravity method
10. Foodborne Illness

48 million Americans affected/year; 128,000 hospitalized; ~3,000 deaths. Bacteria multiply quickly when food temp is between 40°F and 140°F ("danger zone").

Patient Teaching — Preventing Food Poisoning (Table 46.25)

  • Cook ground beef to ≥160°F (use thermometer)
  • Keep raw meat separate from ready-to-eat foods
  • Drink pasteurized milk/juice; wash fruits and vegetables thoroughly
  • Refrigerate food promptly; avoid the 40°F–140°F temperature danger zone

  • Amoxicillin 1 g BID, clarithromycin 500 mg BID, metronidazole 500 mg QID, tetracycline 500 mg QID

Magnesium Antacids — Renal Patients Patients with renal failure should NOT take magnesium preparations (risk for magnesium toxicity).
12. NCLEX Critical To Knows
1. GERD: Lifestyle Teaching Priorities
  • Elevate HOB 30 degrees (4–6 inch blocks or pillows, not just pillow wedge)
  • Do NOT lie down for 2–3 hours after eating; no eating within 3 hr of bedtime
  • Smoking cessation causes almost immediate ↑ in LES pressure
  • GERD chest pain relieved by antacids; angina is NOT — key distinction
  • Barrett esophagus = squamous → columnar metaplasia; precancerous; requires endoscopic surveillance
2. H. pylori and PUD
  • H. pylori: 80% of gastric ulcers, 90% of duodenal ulcers
  • Eradication requires 14 days of antibiotics + PPI (triple or quadruple therapy)
  • Urea breath test and stool antigen test identify active infection; antibody tests stay positive for years — not useful for evaluating treatment response
  • Gastric vs. duodenal ulcer pain: gastric = 1–2 hr after meals (food may worsen); duodenal = 2–5 hr after meals, relieved by food/antacids, wakes at night
3. PUD Complications — Emergency Recognition
  • Perforation (most lethal): sudden, severe epigastric pain → throughout abdomen; radiates to back/shoulders; rigid, board-like abdomen; absent bowel sounds → SURGICAL EMERGENCY
  • GI Bleeding (most common): changes in vital signs + ↑ red NG aspirate; blood neutralizes acid → pain may DECREASE with bleeding
  • Obstruction: projectile vomiting with undigested food from hours/days ago; visible upper abdominal distension; NG aspirate residual >200 mL → hold oral feedings
4. Dumping Syndrome After Gastric Surgery
  • Early dumping (15–30 min after eating): weakness, sweating, palpitations, dizziness, abdominal cramps, borborygmi, urge to defecate — from rapid fluid shifts
  • Late dumping / postprandial hypoglycemia (~2 hr after eating): hypoglycemic symptoms (sweating, confusion, tachycardia) — from excess insulin release
  • Diet: 6 small meals; no fluids with meals; avoid concentrated sweets; lie down after eating
  • Pernicious anemia after total gastrectomy → lifelong cobalamin replacement
5. Upper GI Bleeding Management Priorities
  • Hematemesis assessment: bright red = active arterial bleed; coffee-grounds = blood in stomach for some time
  • Melena (black tarry stools) = slow upper GI bleed; hematochezia with dark red stool may also be upper GI bleed
  • Massive hemorrhage = >1500 mL or 25% intravascular volume
  • Priorities: 2 large-bore IVs, O2, ECG, IV PPI before endoscopy, hourly urinary output
  • Initial hematocrit may be NORMAL — not reliable until 4–6 hr after fluid replacement
6. Achalasia vs. Other Dysphagia
  • Achalasia: dysphagia for BOTH liquids AND solids (distinguishes from esophageal cancer where solids are affected first)
  • Esophageal cancer: progressive dysphagia starting with meats → soft foods → eventually liquids
  • GERD/esophagitis: heartburn is primary; dysphagia suggests stricture formation
  • Zenker's diverticulum: food trapped in pouch → tasting sour food; press on neck to empty
7. Key Drug Points
  • PPIs: take before first meal of day; more effective than H2 blockers for healing
  • Metoclopramide: tardive dyskinesia risk with chronic use — may be irreversible
  • Sucralfate: needs low pH — give ≥60 min before antacids; binds digoxin, warfarin, phenytoin
  • Misoprostol: teratogenic — do not use in women of childbearing age
  • Mg antacids: contraindicated in renal failure (hypermagnesemia risk)
  • Promethazine: parenteral routes only — risk of severe tissue necrosis
8. Oral Cancer Red Flags
  • Any oral lesion NOT healed in 2–3 weeks → refer to HCP
  • Erythroplasia (red velvety patch) — >50% become cancerous; more serious than leukoplakia
  • Leukoplakia (white patch) — 15% become cancerous; precancerous; associated with smoking
  • Highest oral cancer mortality in Black men
  • Radical neck dissection: includes removal of sternocleidomastoid, internal jugular vein, lymph nodes — patient typically has tracheostomy
9. Gastritis — Pernicious Anemia Connection
  • Autoimmune (atrophic) gastritis → immune destruction of parietal cells → loss of intrinsic factor → cannot absorb cobalamin (B12) in terminal ileum → pernicious anemia
  • Chronic gastritis with parietal cell atrophy → pernicious anemia
  • Total gastrectomy also causes pernicious anemia (all parietal cells removed)
  • Treatment: lifelong cobalamin (vitamin B12) injections or supplements
10. Foodborne Illness — Key Distinguishers
  • Staphylococcal food poisoning: RAPID onset (30 min–7 hr); from cream fillings, meat, bakery
  • E. coli O157:H7: bloody diarrhea; risk of HUS especially in young/elderly; NO antibiotics (↑ HUS risk), NO antidiarrheals
  • Botulism: home-canned vegetables; neurologic symptoms (diplopia, paralysis); treat with antitoxin and ventilatory support
  • After any outbreak within 2 hours at a group meal: suspect staphylococcal (meat and milk sources)
13. Review Questions

  1. A nurse is caring for an 85-year-old woman who has been nauseated all day and has vomited twice. What is the priority nursing instruction?

    Show Answer
    Give sips of water and elevate the head of the bed to prevent aspiration. Older adults are at higher risk for aspiration with vomiting. Antiemetics require an HCP order. Gatorade is high in sodium and should be given cautiously. High-protein supplements are inappropriate during acute vomiting.

  2. The nurse is treating a patient with Vincent's infection (acute necrotizing ulcerative gingivitis). Which treatment should the nurse anticipate?

    Show Answer
    Topical application of antibiotics, mouth irrigations with chlorhexidine and saline solutions, physical and mental rest, avoiding smoking and alcohol, and a soft nutritious diet. This is NOT treated with tetanus vaccination (not an infectious disease in that sense), viscous lidocaine (used for herpetic lesions), or amphotericin B (used for fungal infections like candidiasis).

  3. A young adult asks the nurse about behaviors that increase risk for oral cancer. What should the nurse emphasize?

    Show Answer
    Avoiding use of smokeless tobacco (snuff, chewing tobacco). About 75–90% of oral cancer patients report tobacco or alcohol use. Smokeless tobacco directly contacts oral mucosa and is a significant risk factor. Chewing gum, lip gloss, and carbonated beverages are not risk factors for oral cancer.

  4. Which instructions would the nurse include in a teaching plan for a patient with mild GERD?

    Show Answer
    The best time to take an as-needed antacid is 1–3 hours after meals (when needed effects last up to 3–4 hours). Milk at bedtime increases gastric acid secretion. Chewing gum actually increases saliva and may help mild symptoms. High-protein foods do not specifically exacerbate GERD.

  5. A patient who had an esophagectomy for esophageal cancer develops increasing pain, fever, and dyspnea after starting a full-liquid diet. What do these symptoms most likely indicate?

    Show Answer
    Leakage of fluids into the mediastinum (anastomotic leak). Pain, fever, and dyspnea after oral feeding begins are classic signs of an anastomotic leak, which requires immediate treatment to prevent sepsis and death. This is a postoperative complication, not feeding intolerance, tumor extension, or esophageal perforation (which would occur intraoperatively).

  6. The nurse is monitoring a patient with chronic gastritis for pernicious anemia. What is the pathophysiologic reason for this risk?

    Show Answer
    A lack of intrinsic factor normally produced by acid-secreting (parietal) cells of the gastric mucosa. When chronic gastritis causes parietal cell atrophy, intrinsic factor is lost → cobalamin (B12) cannot be absorbed in the terminal ileum → pernicious anemia. This is not direct cobalamin destruction, mucosal blood loss, or RBC degradation.

  7. A patient asks the nurse why peptic ulcers develop. What is the most accurate explanation?

    Show Answer
    PUD is promoted by a combination of factors that cause erosion of the gastric mucosa, including H. pylori infection and certain drugs (NSAIDs, aspirin). While stress, smoking, and alcohol contribute, they are not the primary causes. Ulcers are not caused by stressful lifestyle alone or inherited via Staphylococcus aureus spread.

  8. What should the nurse include in a teaching plan for an outpatient with stomach cancer who is receiving radiation therapy?

    Show Answer
    Wound and skin care, nutrition guidance, medications, and community resources. Patients receiving radiation need skin care for radiation burns, nutrition support for weight maintenance, information about antiemetics, and access to community agencies. Ostomy care is not needed for radiation therapy alone. Alopecia may occur with chemotherapy, not typically radiation to the stomach region.

  9. The nurse is developing a discharge teaching plan for a patient after an acute episode of upper GI bleeding. Which instructions are most important? (Select all that apply.)

    Show Answer
    Key points: (C) Avoiding aspirin and drugs containing aspirin; (D) Only taking drugs prescribed by the HCP. OTC preparations may contain aspirin/NSAIDs. Limiting alcohol to 1 serving/day is not adequate — complete cessation is the goal. Aspirin with milk does not prevent GI bleeding. Taking all drugs 1 hour before meals has no evidence base for preventing recurrent GI bleeding.

  10. Several patients develop nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea 2 hours after eating at a potluck dinner. The nurse should ask specifically about foods containing what?

    Show Answer
    Meat and milk (staphylococcal food poisoning). The 30-minute to 7-hour onset with rapid group exposure strongly suggests Staphylococcus aureus toxin from meat, bakery products, cream fillings, or milk. Home-preserved vegetables suggest botulism (12–36 hr onset). Beef alone could be E. coli (longer onset). Poultry and eggs suggest Salmonella (8 hr to several days).

  11. A patient with achalasia asks why their swallowing problem is different from other esophageal disorders. What is the most important distinguishing feature?

    Show Answer
    In achalasia, dysphagia occurs with BOTH liquids AND solids from the onset. This distinguishes achalasia from esophageal cancer (solid foods first, then liquids as obstruction progresses) and GERD-related strictures (solid food dysphagia). The incomplete LES relaxation and absent lower esophageal peristalsis cause equal difficulty regardless of food consistency.

  12. A postoperative patient who had a Billroth II procedure reports weakness, sweating, palpitations, and abdominal cramping within 20 minutes of finishing lunch. What complication should the nurse suspect, and what is the priority intervention?

    Show Answer
    Early dumping syndrome. The large bolus of hyperosmolar fluid rapidly entering the small intestine after gastric surgery draws fluid into the bowel lumen, causing these symptoms within 15–30 minutes. Priority interventions: have the patient lie down after meals; review diet teaching (6 small meals, no fluids with meals, avoid concentrated sweets). If 2 hours later with sweating/confusion → late dumping syndrome (postprandial hypoglycemia), which requires sugared fluids/candy.
