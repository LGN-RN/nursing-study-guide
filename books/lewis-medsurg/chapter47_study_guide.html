1 · Bowel Elimination Disorders — Diarrhea, Fecal Incontinence & Constipation

Diarrhea

Diarrhea = ≥3 loose/liquid stools per day. Acute ≤14 days; persistent >14 days; chronic ≥30 days. Health care–associated diarrhea begins after 3 days of hospitalization.

Etiology — Acute Infectious

OrganismKey Features
C. difficile (CDI)Watery diarrhea, fever, anorexia; from antibiotics → disruption of normal flora; most serious HAI-associated diarrhea
E. coli O157:H7Bloody diarrhea; can progress to HUS (renal failure); undercooked beef/chicken
NorovirusRapid onset N/V/D; 1–2 days; very contagious; foodborne outbreaks
SalmonellaDiarrhea, fever, cramps 4–7 days; undercooked poultry/eggs
Giardia lambliaMost common GI parasite in US; cramps, N/D; contaminated water/pools
RotavirusProfuse watery diarrhea, fever, vomiting 3–8 days; highly contagious; fecal-oral

C. difficile Infection (CDI) — Special Focus

CDI Isolation Protocol Immediately place in isolation. Gowns + gloves for all visitors and providers. Dedicated disposables. Disinfect with 10% bleach or CDI-sporicidal agent. Alcohol-based hand sanitizers are ineffective against C. diff spores — use soap and water.
  • Spores survive up to 70 days on surfaces
  • Risk factors: antimicrobials, chemotherapy, PPI use, immunosuppression
  • Treatment: vancomycin 125 mg PO 4× daily or fidaxomicin 200 mg PO BID × 10 days
  • Severe/complicated CDI (shock, ileus, megacolon): vancomycin 500 mg 4× daily PO + IV metronidazole
  • Recurrent CDI (~20%): Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT) — infuses donor stool bacteria to restore normal flora

Interprofessional Care — Diarrhea

  • Mild: oral glucose-electrolyte solutions; IV fluids if severe
  • Antidiarrheal drugs (loperamide, diphenoxylate/atropine) — not used in some infectious diarrheas; used cautiously in IBD (risk of toxic megacolon)
  • Antibiotics: fluoroquinolone (ciprofloxacin) or azithromycin — only for severe or immunocompromised
Nursing Management — Diarrhea
  • Oral/IV fluid replacement with electrolytes; record I&O; daily weights
  • Keep perianal skin clean; moisture barrier cream; sitz baths/dibucaine for pain
  • Facilitate toileting: call light in reach, easy clothing, assistive devices
  • Teach food handling, hygiene, infection control

Fecal Incontinence

Involuntary loss of stool. Occurs when normal structures maintaining continence are damaged — motor dysfunction (sphincter weakness) and/or sensory dysfunction (inability to perceive stool).

  • Most common cause in women: obstetric trauma (sphincter injury)
  • Neurologic causes: stroke, MS, diabetes, spinal cord lesions
  • Fecal impaction: liquid stool seeps around hardened feces (common in immobile older adults)

Management

  • Bowel management program: regular defecation, high-fiber diet, caffeine-free fluids
  • Biofeedback training: coordinates sphincters, requires intact sensory/motor nerves
  • Sacral nerve electrical stimulation: improves quality of life, possible complete continence
  • Solesta (dextranomer/hyaluronic acid gel) injection into anal canal — narrows canal
  • Surgery (sphincter repair) or colostomy when conservative measures fail
Nursing: Skin Integrity with Fecal Incontinence Assess for incontinence-associated dermatitis (IAD) — redness, skin loss, rash in perianal area. Distinguish from pressure injury. Use stool management systems (Flexi-Seal, DigniCare) for liquid stool. Do NOT use rectal tubes or urinary catheters as stool catheters (damage sphincter/mucosa).

Constipation

Fewer than 3 stools/week; often straining, incomplete evacuation, hard/lumpy stools. Chronic = >3 months. It is a symptom, not a disease.

Etiology

  • Low-fiber diet, decreased activity, ignoring defecation urge
  • Diseases: diabetes, Parkinson's, multiple sclerosis, hypothyroidism, hypercalcemia
  • Drugs: opioids, antidepressants, antihypertensives, antacids with calcium/aluminum, iron supplements
  • Cathartic colon syndrome: colon becomes dilated and atonic from chronic laxative overuse

Complications

  • Hemorrhoids (from Valsalva maneuver/straining)
  • Valsalva maneuver dangers: transient drop in cardiac output → sudden rise → potentially fatal in heart failure, CAD
  • Rectal mucosal ulcers, fissures, diverticulosis
  • Obstipation + fecal impaction → colonic perforation (life-threatening)

2 · Acute Abdominal Conditions — Pain, Trauma, Appendicitis & Peritonitis

Acute Abdominal Pain & Laparotomy

Pain of recent onset; may be life-threatening. Causes: inflammation (appendicitis, pancreatitis), infection, obstruction, bleeding, perforation. Peritonitis occurs when GI tract perforates and contents irritate the peritoneum.

Emergency Indicators ↑ pulse + ↓ BP = impending hypovolemic shock. Fever = infection. Altered mental status = poor cerebral perfusion. Absent bowel sounds = obstruction, peritonitis, or paralytic ileus.

Patient Positioning Clues

  • Fetal position → peritoneal irritation (appendicitis)
  • Supine with outstretched legs → visceral pain
  • Restless, unable to find comfort → obstruction (kidney stones, gallstones)

Emergency Interventions (Table 47.12 highlights)

  • Patent airway; O2 via nasal cannula or non-rebreather
  • Large-bore IV access; warm NS or lactated Ringer's
  • Labs: CBC, electrolytes, amylase, pregnancy test, type & crossmatch
  • Insert indwelling urinary catheter; NG tube PRN; keep NPO
  • Monitor: VS, LOC, O2 sat, I&O; anticipate surgical intervention

Postoperative Care After Laparotomy

  • NG drainage: dark brown-red first 12 hrs → light yellowish-brown acceptable; bright red = notify HCP (hemorrhage risk)
  • Nausea/vomiting: antiemetics (ondansetron/Zofran)
  • Early ambulation → restores peristalsis, expels flatus, reduces gas pain
  • Progression: clear liquids → regular diet as tolerated
  • Notify HCP for fever >101°F (38.6°C), vomiting, pain, incisional drainage, bowel changes

Abdominal Trauma

Blunt (MVA, direct blow, falls) vs. penetrating (gunshot, stabbing). Solid organ injury (liver, spleen) → hemorrhage → hypovolemic shock. Hollow organ injury (bladder, stomach, intestines) → peritonitis risk.

Abdominal Compartment Syndrome Excessively high intraabdominal pressure. Restricts ventilation → respiratory failure; ↓ cardiac output, venous return, organ perfusion → renal failure. Can follow any edema-producing abdominal event.

Classic Trauma Findings

  • Guarding and splinting of abdomen → peritonitis
  • Hard, distended abdomen → intraabdominal bleeding
  • Cullen sign (bruising around umbilicus) → retroperitoneal hemorrhage
  • Grey Turner sign (bruising on flanks) → retroperitoneal hemorrhage
  • Hematemesis or hematuria → internal organ damage
  • Note: hemoglobin/hematocrit may be normal initially (fluid and RBCs lost equally); deficits appear after fluid resuscitation

Do NOT remove an impaled object — may cause further injury and bleeding; stabilize with bulky dressing.

Appendicitis

Inflammation of the appendix; most common reason for emergency abdominal surgery. Luminal obstruction → distention → venous engorgement → bacterial accumulation → gangrene → perforation → peritonitis.

Classic Presentation

  • Dull periumbilical pain → shifts to right lower quadrant (RLQ) at McBurney's point (halfway between umbilicus and right iliac crest) Classic Path
  • Anorexia, nausea, vomiting follow pain
  • Low-grade fever; rigidity; rebound tenderness; muscle guarding
  • Patient prefers to lie still with right leg flexed

Diagnostic Signs

Psoas sign
Pain with extension of right thigh
Obturator sign
Pain with passive internal rotation of flexed right thigh
Rovsing sign
RLQ pain increases when LLQ is palpated
Risk: Perforation Delay in diagnosis → rupture → peritonitis → potentially fatal. Standard treatment = immediate appendectomy. If ruptured: IV fluids + antibiotics 6–8 hrs before surgery.
Nursing Management: Appendicitis Keep NPO until HCP evaluates (stomach must be empty in case surgery needed). Monitor VS frequently. Give IV fluids, analgesics, antiemetics as ordered. Uncomplicated laparoscopic appendectomy: usually discharged within 24 hours.

Peritonitis

Inflammation of the peritoneum from contamination (bacteria, irritating chemicals). Primary peritonitis = blood-borne organisms enter peritoneal cavity (no intraabdominal cause; e.g., ascites from cirrhosis). Secondary peritonitis = organ perforation releases contents (most common — ruptured appendix, perforated ulcer, diverticulitis, trauma).

Clinical Manifestations

  • Severe, continuous abdominal pain — universal sign is tenderness over involved area Hallmark
  • Rebound tenderness, rigidity, spasm
  • Patient lies still, takes only shallow breaths (movement worsens pain)
  • Abdominal distention, fever, tachycardia, tachypnea, N/V
  • Complications: hypovolemic shock, sepsis, intraabdominal abscess, paralytic ileus, ARDS
Nursing Care: Peritonitis IV access for fluid replacement and antibiotics. Semi-Fowler's or knees flexed for comfort. Antiemetics to prevent further fluid/electrolyte loss. NPO status; NG tube to reduce gastric distention. Accurate I&O; frequent vital signs; oxygen therapy as needed.

Gastroenteritis

Sudden diarrhea, N/V, fever, cramping. Viruses (especially Norovirus) are most common cause. Most cases are self-limiting. Oral glucose-electrolyte fluids prevent/treat dehydration. IV fluids if severe dehydration. Nursing management same as for acute diarrhea.

3 · Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

Chronic abdominal pain with altered bowel patterns (diarrhea, constipation, or mixed). Affects women 2–2.5× more than men. No known single cause. Associated with psychological stressors (anxiety, depression, PTSD), diet intolerances (FODMAPs, gluten), GI infections, family history.

Rome IV Diagnostic Criteria

Abdominal pain ≥1 day/week for 3 months associated with ≥2 of: (1) related to defecation, (2) change in stool frequency, (3) change in stool form.
  • IBS-C (constipation predominant)
  • IBS-D (diarrhea predominant)
  • IBS mixed
  • IBS unsubtyped

FODMAPs

Fermentable oligo-, di-, monosaccharides, and polyols that trigger IBS:

  • Oligosaccharides: wheat, rye, garlic, onions, legumes, some fruits/vegetables
  • Lactose: milk and milk products
  • Fructose: honey, apples, pears, high-fructose corn syrup
  • Polyols: apples, pears, cauliflower, mushrooms, artificial sweeteners (sorbitol)

4 · Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

Chronic GI inflammation with periods of remission and exacerbation. Two forms: Ulcerative Colitis (UC) and Crohn's Disease. ~1.3 million Americans affected. Onset often in teens/early adulthood; second peak in 6th decade.

Etiology

Autoimmune — overactive immune response to environmental/bacterial triggers in genetically susceptible person. Over 200 genes identified. NOD2 gene mutations (Crohn's) prevent normal immune response → bacteria grow unchecked. Smoking worsens Crohn's disease.

Extraintestinal Complications

  • Joints: ankylosing spondylitis, peripheral arthritis, sacroiliitis
  • Eyes: uveitis, iritis, conjunctivitis, episcleritis
  • Skin: erythema nodosum, pyoderma gangrenosum
  • Liver/gallbladder: primary sclerosing cholangitis (can lead to liver failure), gallstones
  • Bones: osteoporosis (bone density scan at baseline and every 2 years)
  • Kidneys: kidney stones; thromboembolism
  • Mouth: aphthous ulcers

Surgical Therapy

Ulcerative Colitis (curative)

  • Proctocolectomy with IPAA (ileal pouch-anal anastomosis) — removes colon/rectum; creates J-pouch or S-pouch; 2-stage surgery; preserves continence
  • Proctocolectomy with permanent ileostomy — when IPAA not feasible

Crohn's Disease (not curative)

  • Resection of diseased segments + reanastomosis — disease often recurs at anastomosis
  • Repeated resections → short bowel syndrome (SBS)
  • Strictureplasty — opens narrowed bowel without resection, preserving intestinal length; low recurrence at site

Nutrition Therapy

  • High-calorie, high-protein, high-vitamin diet
  • During acute exacerbation: liquid enteral feedings (EN) preferred over PN — maintains gut integrity, prevents bacterial overgrowth
  • Iron supplements (oral or IV) for anemia; zinc supplements for ostomies/diarrhea
  • Cobalamin (B12) injections for terminal ileum disease; folate daily with sulfasalazine or methotrexate
  • Calcium + vitamin D supplements with corticosteroids; potassium supplements PRN
Nursing Management: IBD — Acute Care Monitor hemodynamic stability, pain, fluid/electrolyte balance, nutrition. Count and describe stools; assess for blood. Meticulous perianal skincare with plain water + moisture barrier. Dibucaine, witch hazel, sitz baths for irritation. Teach: importance of rest, diet, perianal care, drug side effects, when to seek care, stress management. Refer to Crohn's and Colitis Foundation.
5 · Bowel Obstruction

Intestinal contents cannot pass through the GI tract. May occur in small (SBO) or large (LBO) intestine. Can be partial or complete, simple (intact blood supply) or strangulated (no blood supply).

Types

TypeSBO CausesLBO Causes
Mechanical (physical block)Surgical adhesions (most common), hernias, Crohn's strictures, intussusception, cancerCRC (most common), diverticular disease, adhesions, ischemia, volvulus
Nonmechanical (reduced peristalsis)Paralytic ileus (most common form — occurs after any abdominal surgery); also peritonitis, electrolyte imbalances (especially hypokalemia), acute pancreatitis; pseudo-obstruction (mimics mechanical but no anatomic cause)
VascularRare; emboli/atherosclerosis of mesenteric arteries; venous thrombosis in low-flow states

Pathophysiology

  • Fluid, gas, intestinal contents accumulate proximal to obstruction → distention
  • Increased intraluminal pressure → capillary permeability ↑ → fluid/electrolyte loss into peritoneum → hypovolemic shock
  • If blood flow stops → bowel ischemia → necrosis → perforation → septic shock, death
  • Intestinal strangulation = bowel segment becomes edematous, cyanotic, gangrenous

Nursing Management

  • Monitor for hypovolemic shock, sepsis, strangulation
  • Strict I&O; indwelling urinary catheter (report <0.5 mL/kg/hr = acute kidney injury risk)
  • Rising creatinine and BUN = additional indicators of AKI
  • NG tube for decompression; NPO
  • IV fluids and electrolytes; pain management; check NG tube patency every 4 hours
  • Monitor serum electrolytes and ABGs; report deterioration in vital signs, bowel sounds, or abdominal distention
6 · Colorectal Polyps & Colorectal Cancer (CRC)

Polyps of the Large Intestine

Arise from mucosal surface; sessile (flat, broad-based) or pedunculated (thin stalk). Most are asymptomatic. Rectal bleeding and occult blood in stool are most common signs.

  • Hyperplastic polyps: noncancerous; rarely >5 mm; no clinical symptoms
  • Adenomatous polyps: neoplastic; closely linked to CRC; three types — tubular, tubulovillous, villous. Villous polyps most likely to develop cancer.
  • All polyps are considered abnormal; all should be removed (polypectomy)
  • After polypectomy: watch for rectal bleeding, fever, severe abdominal pain, distention (perforation/hemorrhage)

Genetic Polyposis Syndromes

Familial Adenomatous Polyposis (FAP)
Autosomal dominant; APC gene mutation (tumor suppressor); hundreds to thousands of polyps → inevitable CRC by ~age 40 → prophylactic colectomy by age 25. Also at risk for thyroid, stomach, liver, brain cancers.
Hereditary Nonpolyposis CRC (HNPCC/Lynch Syndrome)
Autosomal dominant; MSH2, MLH1, MSH6, PMS2 mutations (DNA repair genes); 50–80% lifetime CRC risk; also ↑ risk for stomach, brain, ovary, uterus, urinary tract cancers. Colonoscopy every 1–2 years.

Colorectal Cancer (CRC)

2nd leading cause of cancer-related death in US. ~148,000 diagnosed/year; ~53,000 deaths. Higher risk in men; rising incidence in ages 20–49.

Risk Factors

  • Personal/family history of CRC, IBD, diabetes
  • History of FAP, HNPCC; first-degree relative with CRC
  • Obesity (BMI ≥30), physical inactivity, alcohol ≥4 drinks/week, cigarette smoking
  • Red meat ≥7 servings/week; high-fat, low-fiber diet
  • Blacks have highest incidence and mortality; Hispanics/Asians least likely to undergo screening

Pathophysiology

  • Usually starts as adenomatous polyp → grows over 10–20 years → invades bowel wall → lymph node spread → vascular spread
  • Common metastatic sites (via portal vein): liver first → then lungs, bones, brain
  • KRAS gene mutation (30–50% of CRC): oncogene; normal cells → cancerous

Screening Guidelines

Begin CRC screening at age 45 (average risk). Colonoscopy is gold standard — every 10 years. Flexible sigmoidoscopy every 5 years. Annual FOBT or FIT (must do annually — bleeding is intermittent). Stool DNA test every 3 years. High-risk: start screening earlier, screen more frequently.

Carcinoembryonic Antigen (CEA)

Glycoprotein produced by some CRC cells. Used to monitor for recurrence after surgery/chemotherapy, not as a primary screening tool (many false positives — also elevated with pancreatitis, IBD, cirrhosis, COPD, other cancers).

Chemotherapy & Targeted Therapy

  • Adjuvant chemo recommended for stage III (lymph node involvement)
  • Standard regimens: oxaliplatin (Eloxatin) + fluorouracil (FU) + leucovorin (LV); or capecitabine (Xeloda)
  • Angiogenesis inhibitors: bevacizumab (Avastin), aflibercept (Zaltrap), ramucirumab (Cyramza)
  • Epidermal growth factor receptor blockers: cetuximab (Erbitux), panitumumab (Vectibix)
  • Regorafenib (Stivarga) and Lonsurf (trifluridine/tipiracil): for metastatic CRC no longer responding to other therapy

Ileostomy Output Milestones

  • First 24–48 hrs: minimal output
  • When peristalsis returns: output may reach 1500–1800 mL/24 hr → monitor for fluid/electrolyte imbalance (especially K+ and Na+)
  • Over days to weeks: proximal small bowel adapts → stool thickens to paste, volume decreases to ~500 mL/day

Key Nursing Care Points

Preoperative Ostomy Care WOCN should visit before surgery: assess self-care ability, identify support systems, mark best stoma site (within rectus muscle, on flat surface, visible to patient, hidden under clothing). Preoperative education ↓ anxiety and improves postoperative outcomes.
Postoperative Ostomy Care Use transparent pouch initially to assess stoma viability. Empty pouch when 1/3 full (prevents pull from weight). Drainable pouch for ascending/transverse colostomy (up to 4–7 days). Sigmoid colostomy can use drainable or disposable closed-end pouch. Ileostomy: always drainable; increase fluid 2–3 L/day; learn signs of dehydration. Chew food thoroughly (ileostomy blockage risk from nuts, popcorn, mushrooms, stringy vegetables).

Colostomy Irrigation

Option for distal colostomy to stimulate emptying; patient may wear only a pad between irrigations. Requires manual dexterity and adequate vision. Always keep ostomy bags available in case of diarrhea.

Sexual Function After Ostomy

  • Pelvic surgery can disrupt parasympathetic nerve plexus
  • Men: erection/ejaculation problems; APR especially at risk for ejaculation issues from presacral sympathetic nerve damage. May resolve in 3–12 months; nerve-sparing techniques used when possible
  • Women: vaginal dryness, decreased sensation. Experimentation with positions and lubrication may help
  • Teach: empty pouch before sex; smaller pouch or pouch cover available; share support group resources (United Ostomy Associations of America)

After IPAA — Post-Op Specifics

  • Initially 4–6+ stools/day; adapts over 3–6 months (fewer BMs)
  • Transient incontinence of mucus after intraoperative anal canal manipulation — Kegel exercises at ~4 weeks strengthen pelvic floor
  • Perianal skincare: mild cleanser, rinse, dry, moisture barrier ointment; perineal pad
  • Phantom rectal pain/urge to defecate — normal and usually subsides
8 · Diverticular Disease & GI Fistulas

Diverticulosis vs. Diverticulitis

Diverticula
Saccular outpouchings of mucosa in the colon; most common in left (descending/sigmoid) colon; occur at weak points where blood vessels pass through the muscle layer
Diverticulosis
Presence of multiple noninflamed diverticula; most have no symptoms; increased with low-fiber, high red meat/refined carbohydrate Western diet
Diverticulitis
Inflammation of ≥1 diverticulum → perforation into peritoneum

Risk Factors

  • Low-fiber, high red meat diet; obesity; inactivity; smoking; excess alcohol; NSAID use
  • Uncommon in vegetarians

Clinical Manifestations of Diverticulitis

  • Acute pain in left lower quadrant (LLQ) Hallmark
  • Decreased or absent bowel sounds, nausea/vomiting, fever, leukocytosis
  • Older adults: may be afebrile with normal WBC and minimal tenderness
  • Complications: abscess, peritonitis, fistula, intestinal obstruction, stricture, hemorrhage

Interprofessional & Nursing Care

  • Preferred diagnostic test: CT scan with oral contrast
  • Mild acute diverticulitis: clear liquid diet, bed rest, analgesics at home
  • Hospitalization if: severe symptoms, unable to tolerate oral fluids, fever + significant ↑ WBC, immunosuppression
  • Treatment: NPO → clear liquids with improvement; IV antibiotics; IV fluids; analgesics; NG suction; bedrest
  • Surgery (resection ± temporary colostomy) for recurring diverticulitis or complications (abscess, obstruction)
Patient Teaching: Diverticular Disease High-fiber diet (fruits/vegetables) + ↓ red meat + ↓ fat. Fluid intake ≥2 L/day. Avoid factors ↑ intraabdominal pressure: straining, vomiting, bending, heavy lifting, tight clothing. Weight loss if obese. Smoking cessation.

GI Fistulas

Abnormal tract between 2 hollow organs or a hollow organ and the skin. Most occur after surgery or trauma. Also with IBD, cancer, radiation, diverticulitis, pancreatitis. Associated with ↑ morbidity and mortality.

Types

  • Enterocutaneous: small intestine → skin; intestinal contents drain through skin opening
  • Colocutaneous: colon → skin; stool/pus drains through skin opening
  • Colovesical: colon → urinary tract; fecaluria (stool with urination), recurrent UTIs, dysuria, hematuria
  • Enterovaginal: small intestine → vagina; stool and gas drain through vagina

Output categories: High (>500 mL/day), moderate (200–500 mL/day), low (<200 mL/day).

Management

  • Accurate I&O; monitor urine output and fistula output to guide fluid replacement
  • Monitor electrolytes (low K+, Mg2+, PO43– from GI fluid loss)
  • NPO; acid suppression (PPI or H2-blocker); antimotility drugs (loperamide)
  • High-calorie, high-protein PN or EN; trace element supplements (copper, zinc, magnesium)
  • Consult WOCN for skin protection; high-output fistulas need specialty pouches, barrier creams/sealants, negative pressure wound therapy
9 · Hernias

Protrusion of tissue (usually intestine) through an abnormal opening or weakened area in the abdominal wall cavity.

Classification

Reducible hernia
Returns into abdominal cavity manually or when supine
Irreducible (incarcerated) hernia
Cannot be placed back; abdominal contents trapped
Strangulated hernia
Blood supply to trapped contents is compromised → acute bowel obstruction; gangrene and necrosis possible → surgical emergency

Common Types

TypeLocation/FeaturesWho
Inguinal (most common)Point where spermatic cord (men) or round ligament (women) emerges from abdominal wall; bulge in groinMen have 25% lifetime risk; women <5% risk
UmbilicalWeak rectus muscle or unclosed umbilical opening; more common in womenObesity, infants
FemoralThrough femoral ring into femoral canal; bulge below inguinal ligament; easily strangulatesOlder women especially
Ventral/IncisionalAt site of previous incision; obesity, multiple surgeries, wound healing problems; includes peristomal herniasPost-surgical patients

Clinical Manifestations

  • Pain worsened by lifting, coughing, straining (↑ intraabdominal pressure)
  • Visible bulge when tensing abdominal muscles
  • Strangulated hernia: severe pain + symptoms of bowel obstruction (vomiting, cramping, distention)

Treatment

  • Laparoscopic surgery (herniorrhaphy) is treatment of choice; usually outpatient
  • Hernioplasty: reinforcing weakened area with wire, fascia, or mesh
  • Emergency surgery for strangulated or irreducible inflamed hernias
Post-Herniorrhaphy Nursing Care Monitor for voiding difficulty (intake & output, assess for distended bladder). Scrotal edema after inguinal repair: scrotal support + ice bag + elevation. Encourage deep breathing but NOT coughing. Teach to splint incision; keep mouth open if coughing/sneezing unavoidable. Restrict heavy lifting (>10 lb) for 6–8 weeks.
10 · Malabsorption Syndromes

Malabsorption Syndrome

Impaired absorption of fats, carbohydrates, proteins, minerals, and vitamins. Most common disorders: lactose intolerance, IBD, celiac disease, tropical sprue, cystic fibrosis.

Cardinal sign: steatorrhea — bulky, foul-smelling, yellow-gray, greasy stools with putty-like consistency (undigested fat in stool).

Celiac Disease

Autoimmune disease triggered by gluten (protein in wheat, barley, rye). Damages small intestinal mucosa. Affects ~1 in 100 worldwide. Associated with HLA-DQ2 (95%) and HLA-DQ8 (5%) antigens. Also called celiac sprue or gluten-sensitive enteropathy.

Pathophysiology

Gluten → prolamin peptides → absorbed into submucosa → bind HLA-DQ2/DQ8 → activate inflammatory response → damages microvilli/brush border → ↓ surface area for absorption. Damage worst in duodenum. Continues as long as gluten ingested.

Clinical Manifestations

  • Classic: foul-smelling diarrhea, abdominal pain, flatulence, abdominal distention
  • Atypical (no GI symptoms): joint pain, liver problems, fatigue, peripheral neuropathy, reproductive problems
  • Dermatitis herpetiformis: intensely pruritic, vesicular skin lesion on buttocks, scalp, face, elbows, knees
  • Weight loss, muscle wasting, anemia (folate, iron, cobalamin deficiencies), lactose intolerance, osteoporosis

Diagnosis & Treatment

  • Complete testing before starting gluten-free diet (diet alters results)
  • Best serologic test: tissue transglutaminase IgA antibody + IgA antibody
  • Gold standard: intestinal biopsy showing flattened mucosa and loss of villi
  • Treatment: strict gluten-free diet for life (Table 47.38 — avoid wheat, barley, rye; check labels on medications, food additives)
  • Bone density screening every 2–3 years; annual nutrition evaluation

Lactase Deficiency

Lactase enzyme deficient or absent → lactose not broken down into glucose and galactose → undigested lactose in small intestine attracts water → osmotic diarrhea. Most common in Asian and African ancestry. Symptoms: bloating, flatulence, cramping, diarrhea — within 30 min to several hours of lactose ingestion.

  • Treatment: limit lactose (cheese < milk = lower lactose; live culture yogurt = less lactose due to bacteria); lactase enzyme tablets (OTC) with meals; probiotics
  • Supplement calcium to prevent osteoporosis from dairy restriction

Short Bowel Syndrome (SBS)

Insufficient small intestinal surface area to absorb adequate nutrients. Develops with loss of 50–75% of small intestine. Causes: Crohn's, cancer, surgical removal, congenital defects. If terminal ileum, ileocecal valve, and colon intact → rarely have SBS problems.

Clinical Manifestations

  • Dehydration, weight loss, diarrhea, malnutrition, vitamin deficiencies, electrolyte imbalances
  • Steatorrhea, abdominal pain, flatulence; may develop lactase deficiency and bacterial overgrowth
  • Peripheral neuropathy (B12, vitamin E, copper, thiamine deficiencies); anemia (iron, folate deficiency)

Treatment

  • Immediate post-resection: PN to replace fluid/electrolytes/nutrients; rest bowel
  • Gradually reintroduce EN and oral diet → stimulates intestinal adaptation
  • Ideal diet: high protein + complex carbohydrates; low fat and simple sugars; ≥6 small meals/day; oral supplements
  • FDA-approved drugs: somatropin (enhances intestinal adaptation), glutamine (improves absorption), teduglutide (Gattex) — increases intestinal surface area and fluid absorption
  • Antisecretory/antimotility drugs: PPIs, H2-blockers, clonidine, octreotide, opioid antidiarrheals
  • Cholestyramine: reduces diarrhea from unabsorbed bile acids in limited ileal resections
  • Antibiotic therapy for bacterial overgrowth
  • Intestinal transplantation: only long-term option for intestinal failure; can include intestine alone, liver + intestine, or multivisceral
11 · Anorectal Conditions

Hemorrhoids

Abnormally dilated hemorrhoidal veins. Internal (above internal sphincter) or external (outside external sphincter).

  • Risk factors: pregnancy, constipation, straining, diarrhea, heavy lifting, prolonged sitting/standing, obesity, ascites
  • Internal hemorrhoids: painless bright red bleeding with defecation; may prolapse; pain if constricted
  • External hemorrhoids: itching, burning, edema; painful only with thrombosis (blue-purple, palpable at anal orifice); clot may erode → bleeding

Treatment

  • First-line: normalize stool consistency; high-fiber diet + fluids; avoid straining; warm sitz baths 15–20 min 2–3×/day
  • OTC ointments, creams, suppositories (anesthetics, corticosteroids — limit to 1 week); stool softeners; NSAIDs for pain
  • Rubber band ligation: most widely used technique for internal hemorrhoids; constricts circulation → necrosis → sloughs off; local discomfort; no anesthesia needed
  • Hemorrhoidectomy: surgical excision; indicated for marked prolapse, excessive pain/bleeding, large or multiple thrombosed hemorrhoids
Post-Hemorrhoidectomy Care Multimodal analgesia (opioid + NSAID + topical lidocaine or 2% diltiazem/glyceryl trinitrate). Give pain medication before bowel movements. Stool softeners + bulking agents. If no BM in 2–3 days: oil-retention enema. Warm sitz baths; sponge ring relieves pressure. Patient may have rectal packing (removed postoperative day 1 or 2). Patient fears first BM — reassure and pre-medicate. Use pressure relief cushion (not ring/"doughnut") when sitting.

Anal Fissure

Linear tear in anal mucosa; inciting event usually hard stool. Acute (<6 weeks) or chronic (>6 weeks; characterized by skin tags and fibrotic edges). Ischemia from high internal sphincter pressure + poor blood supply → tissue ulceration.

  • Hallmark: severe anal pain worsened with defecation and direct pressure; bleeding (red on toilet paper)
  • Conservative: fiber supplements, fluids, sitz baths, topical analgesics; topical calcium channel blockers (↓ rectal pressure); botulinum toxin injection
  • Surgery: lateral internal sphincterotomy (if conservative fails); risk of incontinence

Anorectal Abscess

Collection of perianal pus. Caused by obstruction of anal glands → infection. Organisms: E. coli, staphylococci, streptococci. Associated with anal fissures, trauma, IBD.

  • Signs: local severe pain, swelling, foul-smelling drainage, tenderness, fever
  • Sepsis is a complication
  • Treatment: surgical drainage; packing with impregnated gauze or Penrose drains
  • Antibiotics for diabetes, cellulitis, or immunocompromised patients
  • Nursing: warm moist heat; daily packing changes; patient more comfortable prone or side-lying; low-fiber diet; teach wound care, sitz baths, hygiene, follow-up

Anal Fistula

Abnormal tunnel from anus/rectum to perianal skin or vagina. 80% from anorectal abscess. Other causes: Crohn's disease, cancer, diarrhea, trauma, radiation.

  • Signs: persistent, bloody, or purulent discharge; stool leakage; patient may need to wear a pad
  • Fistulotomy: HCP opens fistula; granulation fills wound; care same as hemorrhoidectomy
  • Complex fistulas: LIFT (ligation of intersphincteric fistula tract), rectal flaps, setons, plugs, fibrin glue injections

Anal Cancer

  • HPV associated with ~90% of cases; screening with DRE and anal Pap test in high-risk patients (smokers, HIV-positive homosexual men, immunocompromised, women with cervical/vaginal/vulvar cancer)
  • Treatment: combination low-dose radiation + chemotherapy (mitomycin, cisplatin, FU)
  • If rectum involved → surgical removal of sphincters → permanent ostomy

Pilonidal Sinus

Small tract under skin between buttocks (sacrococcygeal area); lined with epithelium and hair; congenital. Moist skin + movement → hair penetrates skin. If infected → pilonidal cyst or abscess.

  • No symptoms unless infected; then pain and swelling at base of spine
  • Treatment: incision and drainage; wound closed or left open (secondary intention); sitz baths; wound packing
  • Patient most comfortable lying prone or on side; avoid contaminating dressing; avoid straining
12 · NCLEX Critical-To-Know Cards
CARD 1 · C. difficile Infection — Isolation & Treatment
  • Immediate isolation (contact precautions): gowns + gloves for ALL providers and visitors
  • Disinfect with 10% bleach or CDI-sporicidal disinfectant — alcohol-based sanitizers are ineffective against spores
  • Hand washing with soap and water (not alcohol gel) for C. diff
  • Treatment: oral vancomycin 125 mg 4× daily or fidaxomicin 200 mg BID × 10 days
  • Severe CDI: vancomycin 500 mg 4× daily PO + IV metronidazole
  • Recurrent CDI → Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT)
CARD 2 · Appendicitis — Classic Presentation & Emergency Priorities
  • Pain path: periumbilical (dull) → RLQ at McBurney's point (halfway umbilicus to right iliac crest)
  • Positive Rovsing sign (RLQ pain with LLQ palpation), Psoas sign, Obturator sign
  • Patient lies still, right leg flexed; coughing/sneezing worsens pain
  • Keep NPO immediately until HCP evaluates (in case surgery needed)
  • Perforation risk if diagnosis/treatment delayed → peritonitis → fatal
  • If ruptured: IV fluids + antibiotics 6–8 hrs before appendectomy
CARD 3 · IBD — UC vs. Crohn's Distinguishing Features
  • UC: mucosa only; colon/rectum; starts rectum → continuous spread; bloody diarrhea + pain; toxic megacolon more common; fistulas rare; curative with total proctocolectomy
  • Crohn's: transmural; anywhere mouth to anus; skip lesions; cobblestoning; cramping + weight loss; fistulas common (up to 1/3); strictures; not cured by surgery — recurs at anastomosis
  • Both ↑ risk for CDI, CRC, osteoporosis (bone density monitoring every 2 years)
  • Biologic agents (anti-TNF): test for TB + hepatitis BEFORE starting; avoid live vaccines
  • Methotrexate: teratogenic — women must avoid pregnancy
CARD 4 · Bowel Obstruction — Key Nursing Priorities
  • 4 hallmarks: abdominal pain, N/V, distention, constipation
  • Proximal SBO: rapid, frequent, copious bilious vomiting; minimal distention
  • LBO: gradual onset, marked distention, obstipation, late or absent vomiting
  • Report urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hr → acute kidney injury risk
  • High obstruction: metabolic alkalosis (vomiting HCl); low obstruction: metabolic acidosis
  • Strangulation = surgical emergency; signs: severe constant pain, fever, sepsis
CARD 5 · Colorectal Cancer — Screening & Key Facts
  • Begin CRC screening at age 45 for average-risk patients
  • Gold standard: colonoscopy every 10 years
  • FOBT/FIT must be done annually (intermittent bleeding easily missed)
  • CEA monitors recurrence after treatment — NOT a reliable primary screening tool
  • Right-sided CRC → occult bleeding → anemia (often first sign); Left-sided → obstruction, hematochezia
  • APR = permanent colostomy (distal rectal tumor); LAR = sphincters preserved (mid/proximal rectum)
  • Stage III adjuvant chemo: oxaliplatin + FU + leucovorin (or capecitabine)
CARD 6 · Ostomy Care — Priority Assessment & Teaching
  • Normal stoma: rosy pink to brick-red; mild postoperative edema (resolves 6 weeks)
  • Dark red to dusky blue/purple = inadequate blood supply; brown-black = necrosis → notify HCP immediately
  • Empty pouch when 1/3 full to prevent pull/leakage
  • Ileostomy: caustic drainage → always wear pouch; fluid 2–3 L/day; monitor Na+/K+; chew foods thoroughly (blockage risk)
  • Sigmoid colostomy: formed stool; may regulate with irrigation; may not need pouch
  • Postoperative ileostomy output: up to 1500–1800 mL/day until bowel adapts → watch for fluid/electrolyte deficits
CARD 7 · Celiac Disease — Diagnosis & Management
  • Autoimmune disease triggered by gluten (wheat, barley, rye) → damages intestinal microvilli
  • HLA-DQ2 (95%) and HLA-DQ8 (5%) — genetic predisposition
  • Complete diagnostic tests BEFORE starting gluten-free diet
  • Best serologic test: tissue transglutaminase IgA antibody + IgA antibody
  • Gold standard: intestinal biopsy (flattened mucosa, loss of villi)
  • Treatment: strict lifelong gluten-free diet; read all labels; bone density every 2–3 years
  • Dermatitis herpetiformis (pruritic vesicular rash) = skin manifestation of celiac disease
CARD 8 · Diverticulitis vs. Appendicitis — Location Differentiator
  • Diverticulitis → LLQ pain (left lower quadrant; sigmoid/descending colon most common)
  • Appendicitis → RLQ pain at McBurney's point (right lower quadrant)
  • Diverticulosis: usually no symptoms; high-fiber diet prevents exacerbations
  • Acute diverticulitis: NPO, IV antibiotics and fluids, bedrest, NG suction
  • Avoid increasing intraabdominal pressure: straining, vomiting, bending, heavy lifting, tight clothing
  • Preferred diagnostic test for diverticulitis: CT scan with oral contrast
CARD 9 · Hernia — Types & Surgical Emergencies
  • Inguinal hernia: most common type overall; men 25% lifetime risk
  • Femoral hernia: women (especially older); most easily strangulates
  • Umbilical: more common in women; obesity; infants
  • Strangulated hernia = surgical emergency: no blood supply → obstruction → necrosis
  • Post-herniorrhaphy: scrotal edema → scrotal support + ice + elevation
  • Restrict lifting >10 lb for 6–8 weeks; encourage deep breathing but NOT coughing; splint incision
CARD 10 · Abdominal Trauma — Key Findings
  • Cullen sign (periumbilical bruising) and Grey Turner sign (flank bruising) → retroperitoneal hemorrhage
  • Initial Hgb/Hct may be normal (fluid and RBCs lost equally); deficits appear after fluid resuscitation
  • Never remove an impaled object — stabilize with bulky dressing
  • Hollow organ injury (bladder, bowel) → peritonitis; Solid organ injury (liver, spleen) → hypovolemic shock
  • Abdominal compartment syndrome: ↑↑ intraabdominal pressure → respiratory failure + renal failure
  • Seat belt injuries: can compress intestines/pancreas against spinal column (blunt force to bowel)
13 · Review Q&A
  1. A patient is admitted with acute infectious diarrhea and a positive culture for C. difficile. What is the most important nursing action regarding infection control?
    Show Answer
    Place the patient in contact isolation immediately. Use gown and gloves for all contact. Wash hands with soap and water — alcohol-based sanitizers do NOT kill C. difficile spores. Disinfect all surfaces with 10% bleach or a CDI-sporicidal agent. Give the patient dedicated disposable stethoscope and thermometer. Treat all objects in the room as contaminated.
  2. A 22-year-old man presents with periumbilical pain that has shifted to the RLQ over the past 12 hours, anorexia, low-grade fever, and nausea. He prefers to lie still with his right leg flexed. What condition do you suspect, and what are the priority nursing actions?
    Show Answer
    This classic presentation indicates appendicitis (periumbilical → RLQ at McBurney's point; patient lying still with right leg flexed; anorexia, N/V, low-grade fever). Priority actions: Keep NPO immediately (stomach must be empty in case surgery needed). Monitor vital signs frequently. Administer IV fluids, analgesics, antiemetics as ordered. Prepare for probable appendectomy. If perforation suspected: IV antibiotics 6–8 hours before surgery.
  3. Compare the key distinguishing features of ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease for the NCLEX.
    Show Answer
    UC: mucosal layer only; colon and rectum; starts in rectum, continuous spread; bloody diarrhea is predominant symptom; toxic megacolon more common; fistulas rare; curative with total proctocolectomy. Crohn's: transmural (all layers); anywhere in GI tract; skip lesions; cobblestoning; crampy pain with weight loss/malabsorption; fistulas common; strictures; NOT cured by surgery (recurs at anastomosis; may lead to SBS from repeated resections). Both share: ↑ CDI risk, ↑ CRC risk, extraintestinal manifestations, osteoporosis risk.
  4. A patient with a bowel obstruction has urine output of 20 mL in the last hour. His weight is 80 kg. What is your interpretation and priority action?
    Show Answer
    Normal urine output is ≥0.5 mL/kg/hr. For this patient: 80 kg × 0.5 = 40 mL/hr minimum. 20 mL/hr is severely below threshold — this indicates inadequate vascular volume and potential acute kidney injury. Priority: report to HCP immediately. Monitor rising creatinine and BUN (additional indicators of AKI). Maintain strict I&O. Ensure IV fluid replacement is adequate. Check NG tube patency (decompression).
  5. A patient with a new sigmoid colostomy reports the stoma looks dark purple with mild edema. What is your interpretation and action?
    Show Answer
    A dark red to dusky blue/purple stoma indicates inadequate blood supply to the stoma or bowel — this is an urgent finding. Notify the HCP immediately. A rose to brick-red stoma with mild edema is normal in the postoperative period (edema resolves in ~6 weeks). Brown-black stoma = necrosis — also notify HCP immediately. Document all stoma color assessments every 4 hours in the early postoperative period.
  6. A 55-year-old man is newly diagnosed with celiac disease. He asks why he can't just "start the diet and then do the tests." How do you respond?
    Show Answer
    Diagnostic testing for celiac disease must be completed before starting a gluten-free diet because the gluten-free diet will cause changes in the intestinal mucosa and serologic markers, potentially giving false-negative results. The best serologic test is the tissue transglutaminase IgA antibody plus an IgA antibody. The gold standard is intestinal biopsy showing flattened mucosa and loss of villi. Once diagnosed, he will need to follow a strict lifelong gluten-free diet — wheat, barley, and rye must be avoided permanently.
  7. You are teaching a patient about CRC screening. She is 46 years old with no family history. What do you tell her about when and how often she should be screened?
    Show Answer
    At average risk, CRC screening begins at age 45. Options include: colonoscopy (every 10 years — gold standard); flexible sigmoidoscopy (every 5 years); double-contrast barium enema (every 5 years); CT colonography/virtual colonoscopy (every 5 years); high-sensitivity FOBT (every year); FIT (every year); stool DNA test (every 3 years). Annual stool testing must be done every year without fail, since tumor bleeding is intermittent. If any abnormality is found on non-colonoscopy screening, colonoscopy is needed.
  8. A patient has diverticulitis. What is the hallmark location of pain, and how does this differ from appendicitis?
    Show Answer
    Diverticulitis characteristically causes pain in the left lower quadrant (LLQ), because diverticula most commonly occur in the sigmoid and descending colon (left colon). This is the opposite of appendicitis, which causes pain in the right lower quadrant (RLQ) at McBurney's point. Remember: "Diverticulitis = Left lower quadrant; Appendicitis = Right lower quadrant."
  9. What is the difference between a herniorrhaphy and hernioplasty, and which type of hernia requires emergency surgery?
    Show Answer
    Herniorrhaphy: surgical repair of a hernia (the hernia is reduced and the defect is closed). Hernioplasty: reinforcement of the weakened area with wire, fascia, or mesh to prevent recurrence. Both are usually outpatient laparoscopic procedures for non-emergency hernias. Emergency surgery is required for: strangulated hernias (blood supply cut off → bowel infarction/necrosis) and inflamed, irreducible (incarcerated) hernias. The femoral hernia is particularly prone to strangulation.
  10. A patient with IBS-D is prescribed alosetron (Lotronex). What critical teaching does this require?
    Show Answer
    Alosetron is a restricted-access drug available only for women with severe IBS-D who have not responded to other therapies. Teach the patient to stop the drug and contact the HCP immediately if she develops: constipation, rectal bleeding, bloody diarrhea, or abdominal pain — these signs could indicate serious side effects of severe constipation or ischemic colitis (reduced blood flow to the intestines). Eluxadoline (Viberzi) is another IBS-D option and is taken daily; rifaximin is given as a 2-week course with repeat courses as needed.
